## v24-01

Interreg, launched in 1990, is the part of EU cohesion policy that funds cooperation across national borders. It is financed mainly from the European Regional Development Fund and has several strands. Cross-border programmes support neighbouring regions on either side of a border, for example to run joint ambulance services or connect public transport; transnational programmes cover larger areas such as the Baltic Sea or the Alps; and interregional programmes let cities and regions anywhere in the Union exchange experience. Every project must involve partners from at least two countries.

Partners from outside the Union, such as Norway, Switzerland or candidate countries, can take part in many programmes, although they usually finance their own share from national or other sources rather than from the Regional Development Fund. Evaluations praise the programmes for building trust between administrations, but note that their budgets are small compared with mainstream cohesion funding.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. An Interreg project can be carried out by a single Member State on its own.
B. Joint ambulance services are the most common type of cross-border project.
C. A Swiss partner's share is normally paid from sources other than the Fund.
D. Interreg has transformed relations between all of Europe's border regions.

## v24-02

Under the EU's Bathing Water Directive of 2006, authorities monitor designated bathing sites throughout the swimming season, testing samples for two types of bacteria, E. coli and intestinal enterococci, which indicate pollution from sewage or animal waste. Each site is classified as excellent, good, sufficient or poor, based on results from the last four seasons rather than a single year. At a site classified as poor, measures must be taken to reduce pollution and the public must be advised against bathing; information about the classification must be displayed near the site.

Each year the European Environment Agency publishes the results, and in recent years around 85 per cent of sites have been rated excellent. The classification concerns water quality only: it says nothing about currents, lifeguards or litter. The Blue Flag, a separate award run by a private foundation, also considers safety and services.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Results from earlier seasons, not just this one, could make a site poor.
B. A site's classification is based on its results in the latest season alone.
C. The European Environment Agency awards the Blue Flag to excellent sites.
D. An excellent rating guarantees that a beach is safe for swimmers.

## v24-03

Weather forecasts are produced by computer models that start from the current state of the atmosphere and calculate how it will evolve. Because the atmosphere is chaotic, tiny errors in the starting data grow over time, a property the meteorologist Edward Lorenz described in the 1960s. Forecasters therefore run the same model many times, each run starting from slightly different initial conditions. The European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts, founded in 1975, runs around fifty such variations twice a day.

If most runs agree, forecasters can be confident; if they diverge widely, the forecast is uncertain. The spread also allows probabilities to be issued, such as a 70 per cent chance of rain, meaning that rain falls in about seven out of ten comparable situations. For day-to-day weather, useful predictions rarely extend beyond about two weeks, however powerful the computers become.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. The Centre was founded in 1975 to test Edward Lorenz's theory of chaos.
B. Ensemble forecasts are generally more accurate for rain than for heat.
C. Agreement among the runs proves that a forecast will turn out correct.
D. Wide disagreement between the runs signals that a forecast is uncertain.

## v24-04

Soil sealing is the covering of land with impermeable materials such as asphalt and concrete, mostly for housing, roads and industry. Sealed soil can no longer absorb rainwater, so more water runs off into drains and rivers during heavy storms, increasing the risk of local flooding. It also stops soil from storing carbon, filtering water and growing food, and because towns have often grown on fertile plains, some of Europe's best farmland has been lost.

Sealing is very difficult to reverse: removing the surface does not quickly restore a living soil. The EU has therefore set an aspiration of no net land take by 2050, meaning that any new land taken for building should be balanced by returning other land to nature or agriculture. Planners can help by reusing abandoned industrial sites, known as brownfields, and by building more densely rather than spreading into the countryside.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Removing asphalt quickly returns a sealed soil to its former condition.
B. Under the 2050 aim, not all new building on open land is ruled out.
C. Most sealed land in Europe is probably used for roads.
D. The EU has banned new building on the fertile plains around towns.

## v24-05

In 1947 Bedouin shepherds searching caves near Qumran, on the north-western shore of the Dead Sea, found clay jars containing ancient scrolls. Over the following decade, archaeologists and Bedouin recovered the remains of more than 900 manuscripts from eleven caves, most of them in fragments. They were written mainly in Hebrew, with some in Aramaic and Greek, and date from roughly the third century BC to the first century AD.

The scrolls include the oldest known copies of books of the Hebrew Bible, about a thousand years older than the manuscripts previously known. Comparing the two showed that some texts had been copied with remarkable consistency, while others existed in noticeably different versions. Who wrote or collected the scrolls remains debated: many scholars link them to a Jewish group at Qumran, often identified with the Essenes, while others argue that they came from several sources.

B. Bedouin shepherds sold most of the scrolls to museums abroad.
C. Some biblical texts may have circulated in more than one version.
D. Scholars have proved that the Essenes wrote all of the scrolls.

## v24-06

The modern Olympic Games owe much to the French educator Pierre de Coubertin, who founded the International Olympic Committee in 1894. The first Games of the modern era were held in Athens two years later, with around 240 athletes from fourteen countries competing in sports such as athletics, cycling, fencing and swimming. All of the competitors were men; women first took part in 1900, in Paris.

Coubertin was not the first to attempt a revival. In Greece, Evangelos Zappas had funded 'Olympic' contests from 1859, and in the English town of Much Wenlock, William Penny Brookes had organised annual Olympian Games since 1850. Coubertin visited Much Wenlock in 1890 and later acknowledged Brookes's influence. What distinguished Coubertin's project was its international ambition: he wanted athletes from many nations to compete, partly in the hope that sport would encourage understanding between peoples.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Women first competed at the Games four years after the Athens Games.
B. Brookes probably helped Coubertin found the International Olympic Committee.
C. The Athens Games of 1896 drew large crowds of spectators from abroad.
D. Coubertin revived the Games solely to promote peace between nations.

## v24-07

Alfred Nobel, the Swedish chemist and industrialist who invented dynamite, left most of his fortune to fund annual prizes when he died in 1896. His will named five fields: physics, chemistry, physiology or medicine, literature and peace, and the first prizes were awarded in 1901. Nobel stipulated that the peace prize should be awarded by a committee chosen by the Norwegian parliament, at a time when Sweden and Norway shared a king, while the other prizes are decided by Swedish institutions.

The prize in economic sciences is often counted among the Nobel Prizes but was not part of the will: it was created in 1968 by Sweden's central bank in Nobel's memory and first awarded in 1969. Why Nobel devoted his wealth to prizes is not fully known. A popular story holds that he was shocked by a premature obituary calling him a 'merchant of death', but historians treat the tale with caution.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. The economics prize was one of the five fields named in Nobel's will.
B. All of the prizes, including peace, are decided by Swedish institutions.
C. Historians have shown that a false obituary made Nobel create the prizes.
D. The first prizes were awarded several years after Nobel's death.

## v24-08

Between 1845 and 1852 Ireland suffered a catastrophic famine after a plant disease, potato blight, destroyed much of the potato crop year after year. About a third of the population depended on potatoes for most of their food. Around one million people died of starvation and disease, and roughly another million emigrated, so that the population fell by about a quarter.

The blight struck potatoes across Europe, but nowhere else did it cause comparable loss of life. Historians point to conditions in Ireland: many tenants farmed tiny plots, owned no land and had few other sources of food or income. Grain and cattle continued to be exported from Ireland during the famine. The British government's relief efforts, including public works and soup kitchens, were constrained by a belief that the state should interfere as little as possible in the market, and were widely judged inadequate.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Potato blight affected Ireland but no other country in Europe.
B. Not all of Ireland's food production was lost during the famine.
C. Most of the emigrants probably settled in the United States.
D. The blight alone explains why so many people died in Ireland.

## v24-09

Antonio Stradivari, who worked in Cremona in northern Italy until his death in 1737, made more than a thousand instruments, of which roughly 650 survive. His violins sell for millions of euros and are widely believed to have an unmatched sound. Many theories have tried to explain why: the density of wood grown during a cold period in Europe, chemical treatments against woodworm, or the composition of his varnish.

Blind tests have challenged the belief itself. In studies carried out in the 2010s, experienced soloists played old Italian violins, including several by Stradivari, and high-quality new ones while wearing goggles that hid the instruments' appearance. Most could not reliably tell old from new, and many preferred a new instrument. Critics reply that such tests cannot capture everything a player learns from an instrument over years, but the studies suggest that reputation shapes how these violins are perceived.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. The violins' famed sound may owe something to their reputation.
B. Stradivari's varnish was made to a secret recipe that has since been lost.
C. Blind tests proved that new violins sound better than all old Italian ones.
D. Every soloist in the tests preferred a new violin to an old one.

## v24-10

Before 1858, news between Europe and North America crossed the Atlantic by ship, which took about ten days. In August of that year a telegraph cable laid along the seabed between Ireland and Newfoundland carried its first messages, including a greeting from Queen Victoria to the American president that took around sixteen hours to transmit. The cable failed after about three weeks, damaged in part by the excessive voltage used in an attempt to speed up signals.

A durable link came only in 1866, when the steamship Great Eastern, then the largest ship afloat, laid a stronger cable. Messages could now cross the ocean in minutes, transforming financial markets, diplomacy and journalism. The early cables were costly to use, however: high charges per word meant that, at first, they served mainly governments, businesses and news agencies rather than private individuals.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. The 1858 cable worked reliably for several years before it failed.
B. The Great Eastern had been built specially to lay telegraph cables.
C. At first, the cable was too costly for most private individuals to use.
D. Queen Victoria's message was probably the first ever sent by cable.
