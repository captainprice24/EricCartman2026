## v09-01-c

In October 1950, shortly after the Korean War broke out, French Prime Minister René Pleven proposed a European army in which West German soldiers would serve under a common command rather than in a German national army. The aim was to meet American calls for a West German contribution to Western defence while avoiding the revival of an independent German military. The treaty establishing the European Defence Community (EDC) was signed in May 1952 by the six members of the Coal and Steel Community, and a draft of 1953 would have placed it within a European Political Community.

After the failure of the defence community, the six coal and steel countries returned to economic cooperation. ## v09-03-c

By the early 1960s three European Communities existed side by side: the European Coal and Steel Community, set up in 1952, and the European Economic Community and Euratom, both operating since 1958. Under a convention signed together with the Rome treaties, a single parliamentary assembly and a single Court of Justice already served all three. Each Community, however, still had its own executive and its own council of ministers: the coal and steel community was run by a High Authority, while the two newer Communities each had a Commission.

The merger concerned the institutions, not the Communities themselves, which remained distinct legal entities, each governed by its own founding treaty. The coal and steel community ended when its treaty expired in 2002, while Euratom still exists today alongside the European Union.

## v09-04-c

The Treaty on European Union, signed in Maastricht in February 1992, created the European Union with a structure often described as a temple resting on three pillars. The first pillar consisted of the existing Communities, where the 'Community method' applied: the Commission had the right of initiative, the Council often decided by qualified majority, and the Court of Justice could enforce the rules.

The second pillar covered a common foreign and security policy; the third covered justice and home affairs, including asylum, immigration and police cooperation. In both, decision-making was essentially intergovernmental: the Council generally acted by unanimity, the Commission either shared the right of initiative with Member States or had none, and the Parliament and the Court played only a limited role. Governments wanted to cooperate in these sensitive areas without handing them over to supranational institutions. Danish voters rejected the treaty in June 1992 and approved it in May 1993, after Denmark had obtained several opt-outs, and it entered into force on 1 November 1993.

## v09-05-c

The Treaty of Amsterdam, signed in 1997, extended the powers of the European Parliament but left unresolved the institutional reforms needed for a much larger Union. These 'Amsterdam leftovers', chiefly the size of the Commission, the weighting of Council votes and the extension of qualified majority voting, were the main business of the intergovernmental conference that ended in Nice in December 2000. The resulting treaty reweighted Council votes in favour of the larger Member States and extended qualified majority voting to a number of further provisions, while unanimity remained the rule in sensitive areas such as taxation. It also provided for a Commission with one member per Member State.

Ireland was the only Member State to put the treaty to a referendum. In June 2001 Irish voters rejected it on a turnout of about one third. After the government obtained declarations on Ireland's military neutrality, a second referendum in October 2002 approved it by a clear majority, and the treaty entered into force on 1 February 2003.

## v09-14-c

In the early twentieth century the astronomer Henrietta Swan Leavitt studied variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud, among them a class now called Cepheids, which brighten and dim in a regular cycle. In 1912 she reported that the brighter Cepheids had longer periods. Because the stars in the Cloud are all at roughly the same distance from Earth, differences in their apparent brightness had to reflect real differences in the light they emit.

This made Cepheids usable as 'standard candles': timing a Cepheid's cycle reveals how much light it gives off, and comparing that with how bright it appears reveals its distance. At first, however, the relation gave only relative distances and had to be calibrated using nearby Cepheids whose distances could be measured independently. In the 1920s Edwin Hubble used Cepheids to show that the Andromeda Nebula lay far beyond the Milky Way. Later work distinguished two types of Cepheid, and correcting the confusion between them roughly doubled estimated distances to other galaxies in the 1950s.

## v09-15-c

The first planet found orbiting a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b, was announced in 1995 by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz, who later shared a Nobel Prize in Physics. They used the radial velocity method: as a planet orbits, it tugs its star slightly back and forth, and the wavelength of the star's light shifts accordingly. This method gives a lower limit for the planet's mass.

Most of the more than 6,000 exoplanets confirmed since then have been found with the transit method, which watches for the slight, regular dimming that occurs when a planet passes in front of its star. A transit reveals the planet's size relative to the star, but it can be seen only if the orbit is aligned almost edge-on from our point of view. Both methods find large planets on close orbits more easily than small, distant ones, because these produce stronger and more frequent signals. Combining the two methods gives a planet's density, and hence whether it is more likely rocky or gaseous.

## v09-16-c

Radiocarbon dating, developed by the American chemist Willard Libby in the late 1940s, relies on carbon-14, a radioactive form of carbon produced in the upper atmosphere. Plants absorb it through photosynthesis and animals by eating plants, so living organisms keep roughly the same proportion of carbon-14 as their surroundings. When an organism dies it stops taking in carbon, and its carbon-14 decays at a known rate, halving about every 5,730 years, so measuring how much remains indicates how long ago death occurred.

The method can be applied only to material that once contained carbon from living things, such as wood, bone, charcoal or textiles. After about 50,000 years so little carbon-14 is left that it can no longer be measured reliably. Because the amount of carbon-14 in the atmosphere has varied over time, raw results must also be calibrated against records of known age, such as tree rings. Modern accelerator mass spectrometry needs only milligrams of material rather than grams, so precious objects can be dated with only minimal damage.

## v09-17-c

Anchoring is the tendency for an initial number to pull later estimates towards it, even when that number is plainly irrelevant. In an experiment reported in 1974, the psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman spun a wheel of fortune, rigged to stop at either 10 or 65, in front of participants, then asked them to estimate the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Participants who had seen 10 gave a median estimate of 25%; those who had seen 65 gave a median estimate of 45%.

Later studies have found anchoring effects among experienced professionals as well as students. In one, estate agents' valuations of a house were influenced by the asking price they were shown, although most of them said the price had played no part in their judgement. Simply warning people about the bias does little to reduce it. Deliberately listing reasons why the anchor might be inappropriate has shown some success, although this weakens the effect rather than eliminating it.

A. Every participant who saw 65 gave a higher estimate than every participant who saw 10.
B. Kahneman's research on judgement later earned him a Nobel Prize in economic sciences.
C. Participants paid for accurate answers were less influenced by the number on the wheel.
D. Not all of the estate agents acknowledged that the asking price had influenced them.

## v09-18-c

Regression to the mean was first described by Francis Galton in the 1880s. Studying the heights of parents and adult children, he found that very tall parents tended to have children who were tall but, on average, less extreme than themselves, and very short parents children who were short but closer to the average. Galton initially read this as a biological tendency towards mediocrity, but the effect is statistical: it arises whenever two measurements are imperfectly correlated, because an extreme result usually owes something to chance factors unlikely to recur.

The phenomenon often misleads people who evaluate interventions. Daniel Kahneman described flight instructors who believed that praise harmed performance and criticism improved it, since trainees praised after an excellent landing usually did worse next time, while those criticised after a poor one usually did better. Both patterns would be expected even if feedback had no effect. Likewise, junctions chosen for speed cameras after an unusually bad year would be expected to see fewer accidents the next year anyway.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Galton's first explanation of the pattern he found in heights was a statistical one.
B. After one very bad year, a school given ineffective help would still be expected to improve.
C. The flight instructors' praise and criticism in fact had no effect on later landings.
D. Regression to the mean means an extreme result is always followed by an average one.

## v09-19-c

Most of a human cell's DNA is packed into the nucleus, but mitochondria, the structures that supply cells with energy, carry a small genome of their own: a circular molecule of roughly 16,600 base pairs, compared with about three billion in the nuclear genome. A cell may contain hundreds or thousands of copies of mitochondrial DNA but only two copies of the nuclear genome. Mitochondrial DNA is inherited almost exclusively from the mother, so it passes down the female line largely unchanged, apart from occasional mutations.

Because there are so many copies, it can often be recovered from old or degraded samples when nuclear DNA cannot. A skeleton found under a car park in Leicester in 2012 was identified as that of King Richard III, in part by comparing its mitochondrial DNA with that of living descendants of his eldest sister through an unbroken female line. Maternal relatives can have matching sequences, however, so a shared match alone cannot identify one individual uniquely.

## v09-20-c

In 1912 the German meteorologist Alfred Wegener proposed that the continents had once formed a single landmass and had since drifted apart, and he set out his case in a book first published in 1915. His evidence was varied: the coastlines of South America and Africa fit together, and similar fossils occur on continents now separated by oceans.

Most geologists rejected the idea for decades. Their main objection was not to the evidence of past connections, which some explained by land bridges that had since sunk, but to the lack of a plausible mechanism: the forces Wegener suggested were shown to be far too weak. He died on an expedition to Greenland in 1930, with his theory still widely dismissed. In the 1950s and 1960s, surveys of the ocean floor showed that new crust forms at mid-ocean ridges and spreads outwards, supplying the missing mechanism. By the end of the 1960s, plate tectonics, which incorporated continental drift, had become the accepted framework of geology.

## v09-21-c

In 1966 Peter Wason devised a simple-looking reasoning test. Participants see four cards, each with a letter on one side and a number on the other, showing E, K, 4 and 7. They are told a rule: 'If a card has a vowel on one side, then it has an even number on the other.' They must turn over only the cards needed to find out whether the rule has been broken.

The correct answer is E and 7: an odd number behind the E, or a vowel behind the 7, would break the rule. The 4 is irrelevant: the rule says nothing about what lies behind an even number. In many studies, fewer than one participant in ten has chosen correctly. Performance improves dramatically when the same structure is presented as a familiar social rule, such as 'anyone drinking beer must be over 18'. Researchers still disagree about why: some argue that people have a specialised ability to detect cheaters, while others stress familiarity with permission rules.
