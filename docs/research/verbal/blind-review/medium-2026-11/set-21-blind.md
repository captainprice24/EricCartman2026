## v21-01

In the early 1980s the European Community produced far more milk than it could sell, because guaranteed prices encouraged farmers to expand, and surpluses piled up in public storage. In 1984 it introduced milk quotas: each Member State received a reference quantity, shared among its producers, and a farmer who delivered more than the quota paid a heavy levy on the excess. Quotas stabilised production for three decades, but quota rights became costly assets, and efficient farmers could not expand without acquiring quota from others.

The system ended in April 2015. Production then rose noticeably in countries such as Ireland and the Netherlands. At the same time, demand from China slowed, and Russia had banned many EU food imports in 2014. Milk prices fell sharply in 2015 and 2016, and the Union briefly paid farmers who voluntarily cut their deliveries.

## v21-02

Smallpox, which killed perhaps 300 million people in the twentieth century, is the only human disease to have been eradicated. By the 1950s it had disappeared from Europe and North America, but it persisted across much of Africa and South Asia. When the World Health Organization intensified its campaign in 1967, mass vaccination proved neither affordable nor sufficient in densely populated areas, so teams switched to surveillance and containment: every case was traced, and the patient's contacts were vaccinated, leaving the virus nowhere to spread.

Several features made eradication possible. The virus infected only humans, so it could not survive in animals and return later. Its visible rash made cases easy to recognise, and one dose of vaccine gave long-lasting protection. The last natural case was recorded in Somalia in 1977, and the disease was declared eradicated in 1980.

C. One dose of smallpox vaccine protected the person for life.
D. An animal host would probably have made eradication harder.

## v21-03

Desalination plants turn seawater or brackish groundwater into fresh water. The most common method today is reverse osmosis, which forces water at high pressure through membranes that hold back salt; it has largely replaced older thermal plants, which boil water and condense the vapour, because it uses much less energy. Even so, desalinated water typically costs several times more than water from rivers or aquifers. The largest users are in the Middle East and North Africa, but Spain also relies on desalination to supply cities and irrigate crops during droughts.

The main environmental concern is brine, the concentrated salt water left over. For every litre of fresh water, plants typically discharge more than a litre of brine, often containing cleaning chemicals, which can harm marine life near the outlet if released carelessly. Diluting it or spreading it through diffusers reduces the damage.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Reverse osmosis displaced thermal plants because it needs far less energy.
B. Desalinated water now costs less to produce than river or aquifer water.
C. A typical plant discharges less brine than the fresh water it produces.
D. Spain mainly uses its desalinated water to irrigate its crops.

## v21-04

Until the mid-nineteenth century, each British town kept its own local time, set by the sun, so Bristol was about ten minutes behind London. Railways turned this into a practical problem: timetables listing each station's local time were confusing, and on single-track lines they could be dangerous. In 1840 the Great Western Railway began running its trains on London time, and by 1848 most companies had followed.

Public clocks did not always change at once. Some towns displayed two minute hands, one for local time and one for 'railway time', and a few resisted for years. Greenwich Mean Time became the legal standard for Great Britain only in 1880. In the United States, the railway companies themselves introduced four time zones in 1883, without waiting for legislation, and in 1884 an international conference chose the Greenwich meridian as the starting point for longitude.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Greenwich Mean Time became legal before the railways adopted London time.
B. Most collisions on single-track lines were probably caused by local time.
C. Not every British town changed its public clocks when the railways did.
D. The railways were the only reason towns gave up their own local time.

## v21-05

In the winter of 1636–37, prices for certain tulip bulbs in the Dutch Republic rose to extraordinary levels before collapsing in February 1637. Rare striped varieties were especially prized; their patterns, it was later found, were caused by a virus that also weakened the plants. The episode became famous through Charles Mackay's 1841 book on 'popular delusions', which described a whole nation ruined by the crash, and economists long cited it as the classic speculative bubble.

Historians who returned to the archives tell a more modest story. Anne Goldgar found few cases of bankruptcy caused by tulips and argued that the trade involved a relatively small group of merchants and craftsmen. Many contracts were never honoured, and courts were reluctant to enforce them, which limited the losses. Some economists even argue that the high prices partly reflected rational expectations about rare bulbs.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Goldgar found that tulip prices generally stayed close to the bulbs' real value.
B. The courts refused to enforce every tulip contract brought before them.
C. Mackay's account may have exaggerated how widely the crash caused ruin.
D. The virus behind the prized striped patterns killed the bulbs it infected.

## v21-06

For much of the twentieth century, bilingualism was thought to harm children's development, and some educators advised immigrant parents to speak only the majority language at home. Later studies reversed that view. By the early 2000s, experiments suggested that bilinguals also outperform monolinguals on tasks requiring 'executive control', such as ignoring irrelevant information, supposedly because managing two languages trains the brain.

That claim has proved hard to replicate. Several large studies found no consistent advantage once income, education and immigration background were taken into account, and a 2015 analysis reported that studies finding an advantage were more likely to be published than those that did not. Defenders reply that effects may appear only in particular groups. What is not disputed is that speaking two languages brings practical and cultural benefits and does not damage children's development.

A. No one disputes that two languages do not damage children's development.
B. Large studies have confirmed a clear executive-control advantage.
C. Immigrant parents often still speak only the majority language at home.
D. The 2015 analysis found that most published studies used flawed methods.

## v21-07

The zebra mussel, a small mollusc native to the region around the Black and Caspian Seas, spread across Europe in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries along newly built canals. In the 1980s it reached North America, probably in ballast water from European ships. It attaches itself in dense clusters to hard surfaces, clogging the intake pipes of power stations and water plants, and a single female can release hundreds of thousands of eggs a season.

Its ecological effects are mixed. Each mussel filters large volumes of water, removing plankton and making lakes clearer. Clearer water lets sunlight reach deeper, which can help water plants, but the loss of plankton deprives native species, including some fish larvae, of food. Since eradication is rarely possible in open waters, authorities concentrate on prevention, such as inspecting boats moved between lakes.

A. Zebra mussels first reached Europe in ballast water from American ships.
B. Inspecting boats has largely stopped the mussels reaching new lakes.
C. Once the mussels are established in open water, removal is impossible.
D. The filtering that clears lakes can also deprive some fish larvae of food.

## v21-08

In 2017 and 2018 Finland tested a basic income. The social insurance institution selected 2,000 unemployed people aged 25 to 58 at random and paid them 560 euros a month, tax-free and without conditions. Unlike unemployment benefit, the payment continued if recipients found work, and they were not required to look for jobs. A control group of unemployed people kept the usual benefits.

The main question was whether removing disincentives would encourage people to take up work. The final evaluation found only a small effect: recipients worked a few more days on average in the second year, and the researchers cautioned that a new activation rule introduced for the control group in 2018 made the comparison harder to interpret. Recipients did report less stress and more confidence in their future. Critics noted that, as only unemployed people took part, the trial could not show what a universal basic income would cost.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Recipients could keep the payment only until they found a paid job.
B. A new rule for the control group may have muddied the result.
C. The trial showed that a universal basic income would be affordable.
D. The experiment proved that a basic income greatly improves well-being.

## v21-09

Esperanto was published in 1887 by Ludwik Zamenhof, an eye doctor in Białystok, where Polish, Russian, Yiddish and German speakers lived side by side, often in mistrust. He believed a neutral second language, belonging to no nation, could ease such tensions, and he did not intend it to replace national languages. Its vocabulary draws mainly on Romance and Germanic roots, its spelling is phonetic and its grammar has sixteen rules without exceptions. Supporters claim it can be learned faster than national languages, although the research behind such claims is limited.

The language attracted an international movement and survived persecution under both Nazi Germany and Stalin's Soviet Union, which regarded its speakers with suspicion. Estimates of speakers today range from tens of thousands to two million, and perhaps a thousand people have grown up speaking it at home.

A. Zamenhof never intended Esperanto to replace national languages.
B. Esperanto is used mainly by people who already speak Romance languages.
C. Research has generally confirmed that Esperanto is learned faster.
D. Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union imprisoned all Esperanto speakers.

## v21-10

By the 2010s many of Europe's overnight trains had been withdrawn. Low-cost airlines and fast daytime trains drew passengers away, sleeping cars are expensive to run, and a night train carries far fewer people than a day train of the same length while paying track charges in every country it crosses. In 2016 Germany's national operator ended its own sleeper services.

The Austrian Federal Railways took the opposite view: it bought part of the German fleet and launched its Nightjet brand, which now links Vienna with cities such as Hamburg, Brussels, Rome and Paris. Concern about the climate impact of short flights has helped the revival, and supporters argue that a night train can replace both a flight and a hotel night. Profitability remains difficult, however, as cross-border services face different national rules and frequent night-time maintenance on the tracks.

A. Germany's operator ended its sleepers after the Nightjet brand had begun.
B. Concern about flying is the reason why night trains are reviving.
C. A night train always saves its passengers both a flight and a hotel night.
D. Part of the fleet behind Nightjet once belonged to Germany's operator.
