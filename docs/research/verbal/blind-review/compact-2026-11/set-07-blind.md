## v07-01-c

EU rules on carbon dioxide emissions from new cars and vans do not set a limit for each vehicle. Instead, they set targets for the average emissions of all the new vehicles that a manufacturer registers in the EU in a given year. A manufacturer's target depends partly on the average mass of its fleet, so a producer of heavier cars is allowed somewhat higher average emissions, and manufacturers may form pools to be assessed together. A manufacturer whose average exceeds its target pays an excess emissions premium of €95 for each gram per kilometre above the target, multiplied by the number of new vehicles it registered that year.

The 2023 revision tightened the 2030 targets to a 55% cut for cars and a 50% cut for vans, compared with 2021 levels. In 2025, after carmakers warned that they faced heavy penalties, the EU allowed manufacturers to show compliance with their 2025 targets on the basis of their average performance over 2025, 2026 and 2027, rather than for 2025 alone.

## v07-02-c

Revised EU rules on the rights of rail passengers have applied since June 2023. When a train arrives at its final destination 60 to 119 minutes late, passengers are entitled to compensation of 25% of the ticket price; for a delay of two hours or more, the figure rises to 50%. Carriers may set a minimum threshold, of no more than €4, below which compensation is not paid. Passengers who were informed of a delay before they bought their ticket have no right to compensation for it.

The revision added an exception. A carrier no longer owes compensation if the delay is caused by extraordinary circumstances, such as extreme weather or a major natural disaster, that could not have been avoided. Strikes by the carrier's own staff, however, do not count as extraordinary circumstances. The exception concerns compensation only: the carrier must still inform and assist passengers and offer them rerouting. Member States may exempt urban, suburban and regional services from parts of the regulation.

## v07-03-c

The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) puts a price on the carbon emitted in producing certain goods entering the EU. It addresses 'carbon leakage': the risk that production moves to countries with weaker climate policies, shifting emissions abroad rather than reducing them. It covers cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity and hydrogen.

During a transitional phase from October 2023 to the end of 2025, importers only had to report embedded emissions. Since January 2026, authorised importers must surrender CBAM certificates priced in line with allowances in the EU Emissions Trading System. A carbon price already paid in the country of production can be deducted from the amount owed. A 2025 simplification exempted importers of up to 50 tonnes of covered goods a year, other than electricity and hydrogen; it removed most importers while retaining the vast majority of embedded emissions. Meanwhile, free allowances for EU producers in the same sectors are being phased out between 2026 and 2034, so that domestic and imported goods face a comparable carbon cost.

## v07-04-c

Emissions from road transport, the heating of buildings, agriculture, waste and small industrial installations are not covered by the EU Emissions Trading System. They fall instead under the Effort Sharing Regulation, whose sectors account for around 60% of the EU's domestic greenhouse gas emissions. Since its revision in 2023, the regulation requires these emissions to fall by 40% across the EU by 2030 compared with 2005.

The EU-wide target is divided into binding national targets ranging from a 10% to a 50% reduction. They were set mainly on the basis of gross domestic product per person, so that wealthier Member States carry a larger share of the effort, with some adjustment to reflect where cuts can be made cost-effectively. Member States can bank surplus allocations, borrow a limited amount from the following year, and buy or sell allocations among themselves. Environmental groups have warned that generous use of these flexibilities could allow some countries to postpone domestic cuts.

## v07-05-c

The Renewable Energy Directive sets the EU's overall target for the share of energy that comes from renewable sources. Its 2023 revision raised the binding target for 2030 to at least 42.5% of gross final energy consumption, with an indicative top-up of 2.5 percentage points that would bring the total to 45%. The target is binding on the EU as a whole rather than on each Member State: countries set out their own contributions in national energy and climate plans, and the Commission can recommend more ambitious efforts if the sum falls short.

Because slow permitting had become a major obstacle, the revision also requires Member States to designate 'renewables acceleration areas' where projects are expected to have limited environmental impact. Renewable energy plants are also presumed to be in the overriding public interest.

## v07-10-c

Adopted in 1991, the Nitrates Directive aims to protect waters from pollution caused by nitrates from agricultural sources, mainly fertilisers and livestock manure. Excess nitrogen that crops do not take up can leach into groundwater or run off into rivers and coastal waters, where it contributes to eutrophication, the excessive growth of algae.

Member States must identify waters that are, or could become, polluted, using among other criteria a threshold of 50 milligrams of nitrate per litre in groundwater and in surface water used for drinking. The land draining into these waters is designated as nitrate vulnerable zones, although a country may choose instead to apply the measures across its whole territory. In vulnerable zones, farmers must follow action programmes that include periods when spreading fertiliser is prohibited and a limit of 170 kilograms per hectare per year on nitrogen from livestock manure. The Commission may grant derogations allowing higher amounts in specific conditions, for instance on grassland with a long growing season.

The EU's Waste Framework Directive ranks ways of dealing with waste in a 'waste hierarchy'. Prevention comes first, followed by preparing for re-use, recycling, other recovery such as energy recovery, and finally disposal, for example in landfill. ## v07-17-c

Nitrogen makes up about 78% of the atmosphere, yet plants cannot use it in its gaseous form. Before the twentieth century, farmers depended on manure, on legumes, whose root bacteria fix nitrogen, and on imported deposits such as Chilean saltpetre and guano. In 1909 the German chemist Fritz Haber showed in the laboratory that ammonia could be synthesised from nitrogen and hydrogen at high temperature and pressure, with the help of a catalyst. Carl Bosch, an engineer at BASF, turned the method into an industrial process, and the first plant began production in 1913. Both men later received Nobel Prizes in Chemistry. During the First World War, which broke out in 1914, the process was also used to supply nitrates for explosives.

Today the Haber–Bosch process supplies most of the nitrogen in synthetic fertilisers, and some researchers estimate that around half of the world's population depends on food grown with it. Because its hydrogen comes mostly from natural gas, it is a significant source of carbon dioxide.

## v07-18-c

A solar cell converts light into electricity when photons free electrons in a semiconductor. Each semiconductor has a band gap, the minimum energy a photon must carry to free an electron. Photons with less energy than the band gap pass through without producing current, while photons with more energy produce current but lose the excess as heat. This trade-off sets a ceiling on efficiency.

In 1961 William Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser calculated this ceiling for a conventional cell made of a single semiconductor under unconcentrated sunlight: about 33%, reached with a band gap of around 1.3 to 1.4 electronvolts. Silicon, which dominates the market, has a band gap of about 1.1 electronvolts and a theoretical maximum of around 29%. The limit does not apply to tandem cells, which stack materials with different band gaps. Some, combining silicon with a perovskite layer, have exceeded 33% in the laboratory, although questions remain about how well perovskites withstand years of outdoor use.

A. Perovskite layers have been found unable to withstand years of use outdoors in tandem cells.
B. Converting 36% of unconcentrated sunlight exceeds the stated single-semiconductor ceiling.
C. Silicon dominates the market because it is cheaper to make than other suitable materials.
D. Shockley and Queisser predicted in 1961 that stacked cells would one day beat their limit.

## v07-19-c

Over the past few million years, Earth's climate has swung repeatedly between glacial periods, when ice sheets covered much of North America and northern Europe, and warmer interglacials such as the present one. In the early twentieth century Milutin Milanković proposed that these cycles were paced by regular changes in Earth's orbit and axis. The shape of the orbit varies over roughly 100,000 years. The tilt of the axis varies between about 22.1 and 24.5 degrees over around 41,000 years; a greater tilt makes summers warmer and winters colder in both hemispheres. A slow wobble of the axis shifts the season in which Earth is closest to the Sun.

These changes barely alter the total sunlight Earth receives, but they redistribute it between seasons and latitudes. What appears to matter most is summer sunshine at high northern latitudes, which decides whether winter snow survives to build ice sheets. Scientists stress, however, that orbital changes act as a pacemaker: feedbacks involving carbon dioxide and the reflectivity of ice amplify their effect.

B. Changes in Earth's orbit and axis alone explain the full scale of the glacial cycles.
C. The present interglacial will last for tens of thousands of years, whatever humans do.

## v07-20-c

Grey wolves were eliminated from Yellowstone National Park in the United States by the 1920s. In 1995 and 1996, 31 wolves captured in Canada were released in the park, and the population grew quickly. The reintroduction has become a much-cited example of a trophic cascade, in which a top predator affects plants indirectly through the animals it eats. In the popular version, wolves reduced elk numbers and made elk avoid risky places such as river banks, so that willows recovered, beavers returned and stream banks stabilised.

Many ecologists consider this account too simple. Elk numbers were also reduced by human hunting outside the park, by drought and by other predators such as bears and cougars, whose numbers rose during the same period. Some field studies tracking individual elk found little evidence that they avoided areas where wolves were most active. Most scientists agree that wolves have affected the park's ecosystem, but the strength of the effect remains disputed.

## v07-21-c

In the 1880s, two systems competed to supply electricity in the United States. Thomas Edison's companies promoted direct current (DC), which flows in one direction, while George Westinghouse, using patents licensed from Nikola Tesla, backed alternating current (AC), which reverses direction many times a second. In this 'war of the currents', Edison's side publicly stressed the dangers of high-voltage AC.

AC won mainly because of the transformer, which could raise its voltage for transmission and lower it again for safe use in homes. Transmitting power at high voltage reduces the current needed and therefore the energy lost as heat in the wires. DC did not disappear, however. Since the mid-twentieth century, high-voltage direct current (HVDC) links have been built where they perform better than AC, notably over very long distances and for undersea cables. Because the converter stations at each end are expensive, HVDC is generally chosen only when a link is long enough for its lower line losses to outweigh that cost.

A. Edison's campaign against AC may have delayed its adoption in some American cities.
B. HVDC links have now replaced AC for long-distance power transmission across the world.
C. Tesla had worked for one of Edison's companies before he licensed his patents.
D. All else being equal, a higher transmission voltage cuts the energy wasted as heat.

## v07-22-c

In 1958 the American scientist Charles David Keeling began measuring the concentration of carbon dioxide in the air at Mauna Loa, a volcano in Hawaii, where the air is well mixed and little affected by local sources. The measurements, which continue today, form the record known as the Keeling Curve. The first readings were around 315 parts per million (ppm); the concentration passed 400 ppm in 2013 and now exceeds 420 ppm. The rise has not been steady: the annual increase in recent decades has been larger than in the 1960s.

On top of the long-term rise is a seasonal cycle. The concentration falls during the northern growing season, from late spring to early autumn, as plants in the Northern Hemisphere take up carbon dioxide, and rises again in autumn and winter, when decomposition releases it. Measurements at other stations, including one at the South Pole, show a similar long-term rise but a much smaller seasonal swing.
