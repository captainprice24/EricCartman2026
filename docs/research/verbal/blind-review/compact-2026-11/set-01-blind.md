## v01-01-c

The European Ombudsman investigates complaints about maladministration in the institutions, bodies, offices and agencies of the European Union. Maladministration is understood broadly: it covers not only unlawful acts but also unfairness, unjustified delays and failure to reply. Any EU citizen or resident, and any company or association with a registered office in a Member State, may complain, normally within two years of becoming aware of the problem. The complainant must first have tried to resolve the matter with the institution concerned. Complaints about national, regional or local authorities fall outside the remit, even when those authorities are implementing EU law, and the Court of Justice acting in its judicial role cannot be investigated either.

The Ombudsman cannot issue binding decisions. Instead, the office may propose a solution, make recommendations and, if an institution does not respond satisfactorily, submit a special report to the Parliament. In practice, much of its influence comes from the publicity that such findings attract.

## v01-03-c

Before adopting the euro, an EU Member State must meet the convergence criteria. Its inflation rate and long-term interest rates must not exceed those of the three best-performing Member States on price stability by more than 1.5 and 2 percentage points respectively. Its government deficit must not exceed 3% of GDP, and its debt must not exceed 60% of GDP unless the ratio is diminishing sufficiently and approaching that reference value. The country must also have taken part in the Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM II) for at least two years without severe tensions, and its legislation must be compatible with the Treaties.

Denmark negotiated an opt-out and is not obliged to adopt the euro. The other Member States outside the euro area are legally committed to joining once they meet the criteria, but because participation in ERM II is voluntary, a country can in practice delay adoption by staying out of the mechanism. Croatia became the twentieth member of the euro area in January 2023.

C. Bulgaria followed Croatia into the euro area and became its twenty-first member.
D. Every Member State outside the euro area is legally obliged to adopt the euro eventually.

## v01-04-c

Adopted in 2019, the EU Directive on single-use plastics targets the ten single-use plastic items most often found on European beaches, together with fishing gear. The measures depend on whether sustainable alternatives exist. Where alternatives are readily available and affordable, products such as plastic cutlery, plates, straws and cotton bud sticks were banned from the EU market from July 2021. For products without such alternatives, including tobacco filters and wet wipes, the Directive relies on other tools. Producers of these products must contribute to the costs of waste management and of cleaning up litter, and certain products must carry a label on the plastic they contain and how to dispose of them.

Covered plastic beverage bottles of up to three litres face specific targets: PET bottles must contain at least 25% recycled plastic by 2025, and all such bottles 30% by 2030. ## v01-05-c

Tardigrades, sometimes called water bears, are microscopic animals rarely longer than half a millimetre. More than 1,000 species have been described, living in habitats from deep-sea sediments to the damp moss on city walls. They are famous for surviving extreme cold, intense radiation and prolonged drying out.

Their main survival strategy is a state known as cryptobiosis. As its surroundings dry out, a tardigrade curls into a compact 'tun', loses almost all of its body water and reduces its metabolism to an almost undetectable level. In this state some individuals have revived after years, and in a 2007 experiment some even survived exposure to the vacuum of space. Researchers have also identified proteins that appear to protect tardigrade cells and DNA while they are dried out. Strictly speaking, however, tardigrades are not extremophiles: they merely endure extreme conditions until normal ones return, and active tardigrades are no more resistant than many other small invertebrates.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. More than 1,000 tardigrade species have been shown to survive the vacuum of space.
B. Tardigrade proteins will probably allow scientists to store human cells in a dried state.
C. Their toughness depends largely on the dried-out tun state, not on their active life.
D. Once in the tun state, a tardigrade survives every hazard it meets, however long it lasts.

## v01-06-c

Most EU legislation is adopted through the ordinary legislative procedure. The European Commission submits a proposal, and the European Parliament and the Council act as co-legislators with equal standing: neither can adopt the act without the other. If they still disagree after the Parliament's second reading, a conciliation committee with an equal number of representatives from each is convened to draft a joint text, which must then be approved by both. If either rejects it, the proposal falls. In practice, most files are now agreed at first reading, often after informal 'trilogue' negotiations between the three institutions. Critics argue that trilogues make it harder for citizens to follow how legislation is shaped, while supporters point to the speed of the agreements reached.

In the Council, most decisions under the procedure are taken by qualified majority, which requires 55% of Member States representing at least 65% of the EU population. A blocking minority must include at least four Member States.

C. The conciliation committee is usually chaired by a representative of the Commission.
D. Trilogues have shifted influence over legislation away from the Parliament to the Council.

## v01-07-c

Around four out of five crop and wildflower species in the EU depend, at least in part, on pollination by animals. Pollinators include not only managed honeybees but also wild bees, hoverflies, butterflies, moths and some beetles, and for several crops wild insects are at least as effective as honeybees. Yet many of these species are in decline: around one in ten bee and butterfly species in Europe is threatened with extinction, and for many others the data are too poor to assess their status. The main drivers include changes in land use, intensive farming, pesticide use, invasive alien species, pathogens and climate change. Because these pressures interact, the loss is difficult to attribute to any single cause.

## v01-08-c

The Schengen Agreement was signed in 1985 by Belgium, France, Germany, Luxembourg and the Netherlands. It was followed in 1990 by a convention setting out how internal border controls would be abolished and what common rules would compensate for their removal, including a shared visa policy, police cooperation and a common database, the Schengen Information System. The rules were incorporated into EU law by the Treaty of Amsterdam in 1999. The area now includes most EU Member States and four non-EU countries. Member States that wish to join must first be assessed as applying the rules correctly, and their full membership requires a unanimous decision of the Council.

A member may temporarily reintroduce internal border checks as a last resort in the event of a serious threat to public policy or internal security. Such controls must be notified, limited in time and proportionate. Several countries have repeatedly extended controls in recent years, prompting debate about whether temporary measures have become permanent in practice.

## v01-09-c

## v01-10-c

On 9 May 1950, French Foreign Minister Robert Schuman proposed placing French and West German coal and steel production under a common High Authority, within an organisation open to other European countries. Coal and steel were the raw materials of armaments, and the aim was to make war between historic rivals 'not merely unthinkable, but materially impossible'. The plan had been prepared largely by Jean Monnet, who became the first president of the High Authority.

The Treaty of Paris, signed in 1951 by six countries, established the European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC). The United Kingdom was invited to take part but declined, unwilling to transfer control of its industries to a supranational body. Unlike most later treaties, the Treaty of Paris was concluded for a limited period of fifty years; it expired in 2002, and the ECSC's remaining activities and assets were taken over by the European Community. Today, 9 May is celebrated as Europe Day in recognition of Schuman's declaration.

The European Union has 24 official languages. Any citizen may write to an EU institution in any of these languages and is entitled to a reply in the same language. Regulations are published in all official languages, and the different language versions of EU legislation are equally authentic: no single version prevails over the others. ## v01-13-c

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) occurs when bacteria, viruses, fungi or parasites change over time and no longer respond to the medicines designed to kill them. It is a natural process, but it is accelerated by the overuse and misuse of antimicrobials in human medicine and animal husbandry. Antibiotics are ineffective against viral infections such as colds and influenza, yet they are still frequently taken for them, sometimes because patients expect a prescription and sometimes because doctors cannot quickly rule out a bacterial infection. Faster diagnostic tests could therefore reduce unnecessary use, although they are not yet available for many conditions.

In farming, the EU banned antibiotics as growth promoters in animal feed in 2006, and since 2022 it has also restricted their routine preventive use in groups of healthy animals. Developing new antibiotics is scientifically difficult and commercially unattractive, because new drugs that work against resistant bacteria are likely to be held in reserve and sold in small quantities.

## v01-14-c

The European Climate Law, adopted in 2021, made the EU's objective of climate neutrality by 2050 legally binding. Climate neutrality means that any remaining greenhouse gas emissions are balanced by removals, for example by forests, soils or carbon capture technologies, so that net emissions are zero. The objective applies to the Union as a whole rather than to each Member State individually.

The Law also sets an intermediate target: by 2030, net greenhouse gas emissions must be reduced by at least 55% compared with 1990 levels. Because the target is expressed in net terms, removals count towards it, but the Law caps their contribution so that the target cannot be met mainly by relying on natural sinks. It also created a European Scientific Advisory Board on Climate Change, an independent body that advises on measures and targets. Critics argued that the 55% target was not ambitious enough to meet the goals of the Paris Agreement, while some industry groups warned that it would raise costs.

## v01-15-c

Galileo is the European Union's global satellite navigation system. Unlike the American GPS and the Russian GLONASS, which were developed under military control, Galileo was designed from the start as a civilian system, although it also offers an encrypted service reserved for government-authorised users such as emergency services and the armed forces. It began offering initial services in December 2016, after years of delays and cost increases.

Galileo is interoperable with GPS, so receivers can combine signals from both constellations, which improves accuracy and availability, particularly in cities where tall buildings block part of the sky. The system also supports a search and rescue service that can confirm to people in distress that their alert has been received. Because Galileo is owned by the EU, its signals do not depend on decisions taken by another country. Precise timing signals from satellite systems are also used to synchronise telecommunications networks, energy grids and financial transactions.

## v01-16-c

Research on learning consistently finds that retrieval practice, which means actively recalling information, for instance by testing oneself, leads to better long-term retention than simply rereading material. The benefit appears even when the test is not marked and no feedback is given, although feedback increases it further. Curiously, learners often feel less confident after retrieval practice than after rereading, because recalling information is effortful and exposes gaps, and many therefore prefer the less effective method.

Timing matters too. Spreading study sessions over days or weeks, known as spaced practice, tends to produce more durable learning than concentrating the same amount of study into one session. 'Cramming' can improve performance in a test taken shortly afterwards, but the material is forgotten more quickly. The best gap between sessions seems to depend on how long the information needs to be remembered. Some researchers also recommend mixing different types of problem within a session, which feels harder but in several studies improved results.

A. Cramming can improve scores in a test taken soon afterwards because it reduces anxiety.
B. Retrieval practice only helps learners when they are told whether their answers are right.
C. How confident learners feel can be a misleading guide to which study method works best.
D. Spaced practice works best when the study sessions are held exactly one week apart.

## v01-18-c

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) has applied since 25 May 2018. It covers organisations established in the EU and also those outside it that offer goods or services to, or monitor the behaviour of, people in the EU. Personal data may be processed only on one of the legal bases the Regulation lists, of which consent is just one; others include the performance of a contract and compliance with a legal obligation.

When a personal data breach is likely to result in a risk to people's rights and freedoms, the organisation must notify the supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours. The individuals affected must also be informed if the risk is high. Infringements can be punished with fines of up to €20 million or 4% of the company's total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Organisations operating in several Member States generally deal with a single 'lead' authority, and critics argue that this concentrates enforcement against large technology companies in a few countries.

## v01-19-c

Natura 2000 is a network of protected areas covering Europe's most valuable and threatened species and habitats. It covers around 18% of the EU's land area and more than 8% of its marine territory, and it is based on two laws: the Birds Directive of 1979 and the Habitats Directive of 1992. Member States propose sites, and those designated under the Habitats Directive are selected on the basis of scientific criteria in consultation with the European Commission.

Natura 2000 sites are not strict nature reserves. Much of the land is privately owned, and farming, forestry and tourism are not banned; they must, however, be managed so that they do not harm the species and habitats the sites are designated to protect. Any plan or project likely to have a significant effect on a site must undergo an 'appropriate assessment', and it may be approved despite a negative assessment only for imperative reasons of overriding public interest and if compensatory measures are taken.

## v01-20-c

Under EU air passenger rules, travellers may be entitled to fixed compensation of €250, €400 or €600, depending on flight distance, if their flight is cancelled less than 14 days before departure. The rules do not expressly grant compensation for delays, but in 2009 the Court of Justice ruled that passengers who reach their final destination three hours or more late are entitled to the same compensation.

No compensation is due if the airline can prove that the disruption was caused by extraordinary circumstances that could not have been avoided even with all reasonable measures, such as severe weather. Technical problems discovered during routine maintenance have generally not been accepted as extraordinary. Separately, airlines must provide meals, refreshments and, where necessary, hotel accommodation while passengers wait, whatever the cause of the disruption. The rules cover all flights departing from an EU airport, and flights arriving from a non-EU country when operated by an EU airline. A 2013 proposal to raise the delay thresholds has been the subject of long negotiations.
