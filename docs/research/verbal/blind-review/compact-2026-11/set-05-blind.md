## v05-01-c

The Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union brings together civil, political, economic and social rights in a single text. Since the Treaty of Lisbon it has had the same legal value as the Treaties. Under its Article 51, it is addressed to the Union's institutions and bodies, and to the Member States only when they are implementing Union law. The Court of Justice has read that phrase broadly: in its 2013 Åkerberg Fransson judgment, it held that Swedish penalties for VAT fraud fell within the scope of EU law, because the VAT system is governed by EU rules. Where a national measure has no connection with EU law, by contrast, the Charter does not apply, and protection must come from other sources, such as national constitutions or the European Convention on Human Rights.

The Charter also distinguishes rights from principles. Principles, such as environmental protection, can be invoked before a court only to interpret or review the acts that implement them, not as free-standing claims.

C. Against a national measure unconnected with EU law, people must look beyond the Charter.
D. A person may rely on the Charter's environmental principle in court as a claim in itself.

## v05-02-c

The European Arrest Warrant, in use since 2004, replaced the slow and politically controlled extradition procedures that previously applied between EU Member States. A warrant issued by a judicial authority in one Member State must, in principle, be executed in another, and the decision to surrender a person is taken by judges and prosecutors rather than by governments. For 32 categories of serious offences, including terrorism, trafficking in human beings and corruption, the executing authority may not check whether the conduct is also a crime under its own law, provided that the offence carries a maximum penalty of at least three years in the issuing state. For all other offences, this 'double criminality' check remains possible.

Member States can no longer refuse to surrender their own nationals simply because of their nationality. If the person consents to surrender, the final decision should be taken within ten days of that consent; otherwise the limit is sixty days after the arrest.

B. A warrant from a judge in one Member State must always be executed by the receiving state.
C. The 32 categories were chosen because they account for most warrants issued each year.
D. A person who consents to surrender is usually handed over within ten days of arrest.

## v05-03-c

Eurojust, the European Union Agency for Criminal Justice Cooperation, was set up in 2002 to help national authorities deal with serious cross-border crime, and since 2019 it has operated under a regulation that gave it its current name and status. Based in The Hague, it brings together one national member from each Member State, usually an experienced prosecutor or judge.

Eurojust does not carry out investigations or bring prosecutions itself; those tasks remain the responsibility of national authorities. Its value lies in coordination. When investigators in several countries are pursuing the same criminal network, Eurojust can host coordination meetings, help resolve conflicts of jurisdiction and support the execution of European Arrest Warrants. It also supports joint investigation teams, in which police and prosecutors from two or more countries work together for a set period under a written agreement. The agency's caseload has grown considerably over the past two decades, and a large share of its cases concern fraud, drug trafficking and money laundering.

## v05-04-c

Europol, the European Union Agency for Law Enforcement Cooperation, supports national police forces in tackling serious international crime and terrorism. It began full operations in 1999, and its current mandate rests on a regulation that has applied since 2017 and was strengthened in 2022. Each Member State sends liaison officers who provide a direct channel to their national authorities.

Europol is not a European police force. Its officers have no executive powers: they cannot arrest suspects, search premises or open investigations on their own initiative in a Member State. What the agency offers instead is information and expertise. It cross-checks large volumes of data supplied by national authorities and partners to detect links between cases, and it produces operational analysis and threat assessments. It can also send experts to support investigations on the ground, with the agreement of the Member State concerned. The 2022 reform widened its ability to process complex datasets and to cooperate with private parties, which data-protection advocates argued called for stronger safeguards.

## v05-05-c

The Dublin rules determine which single Member State is responsible for examining an application for international protection lodged in the EU. Responsibility is allocated according to a hierarchy of criteria, applied in order. Family ties come first: an unaccompanied child is normally the responsibility of the country where a family member is legally present, provided this is in the child's best interests. Next come residence documents and visas, so that a state which issued a valid visa is generally responsible. Only after these criteria does irregular entry come into play, making the country through which the applicant first crossed the external border responsible.

Transfers are not automatic. In 2011, the Court of Justice ruled that an applicant may not be transferred to a Member State where systemic deficiencies in the asylum procedure and reception conditions create a real risk of inhuman or degrading treatment. In 2026, under the Pact on Migration and Asylum, the 2013 Dublin regulation was replaced by new rules that keep the same basic logic.

B. A state that issued a valid visa may be responsible despite irregular entry elsewhere.
C. After the 2011 ruling, Member States suspended transfers to Greece for several years.
D. The 2026 rules under the Pact abandoned the hierarchy for allocating responsibility.

## v05-06-c

The Pact on Migration and Asylum, adopted in May 2024 and designed to apply from 2026, includes a new solidarity mechanism intended to share responsibility between Member States more evenly than the previous rules did. The mechanism is often described as mandatory but flexible. Each year, the Commission assesses which Member States are under migratory pressure and proposes the size of an annual solidarity pool, which the Council then establishes. Member States must contribute their fair share, although those under migratory pressure can ask for a reduction, and each chooses how to contribute: by relocating applicants from countries under pressure, by making financial contributions, or by funding operational and capacity-building measures. No Member State can be obliged to carry out relocations.

Supporters argue that this flexibility made agreement possible after years of deadlock. Critics on one side say that allowing states to pay instead of relocating weakens the principle of shared responsibility; critics on the other side object to any compulsory element at all.

## v05-07-c

The Temporary Protection Directive was adopted in 2001, after the conflicts in the former Yugoslavia, for situations in which large numbers of displaced people arrive at once and normal asylum procedures risk being overwhelmed. It provides immediate, collective protection without the need to examine each case individually. For more than twenty years, however, it was not used: calls to activate it during earlier crises did not lead to a decision.

That changed on 4 March 2022, when the Council, acting on a proposal from the Commission, activated the directive for people fleeing the Russian invasion of Ukraine. Beneficiaries receive a residence permit, the right to work, and access to accommodation, medical care and social welfare assistance, and children have access to education on the same terms as nationals of the host country. The Council has extended it several times, but it remains temporary by design, and Member States are discussing how beneficiaries might move to other forms of legal status when it ends.

## v05-08-c

The EU Blue Card is a combined work and residence permit for highly qualified workers from outside the EU. ## v05-09-c

Under the 2004 Citizens' Rights Directive, the right of an EU citizen to reside in another Member State is graded according to the length of stay. For up to three months, a valid identity card or passport is enough. Beyond three months, the right depends on the person's situation: workers and self-employed people qualify, while students and economically inactive people must have sufficient resources not to become a burden on the host state's social assistance system, as well as comprehensive sickness insurance. Host states may require registration with the authorities after three months.

After five years of continuous legal residence, a citizen acquires the right of permanent residence, which is no longer subject to these conditions. Family members, including those who are not EU citizens, enjoy rights derived from the citizen's. Protection against expulsion also grows over time: once a citizen has resided in the host state for the previous ten years, expulsion is possible only on imperative grounds of public security.

## v05-10-c

An EU citizen travelling outside the Union, in a country where their own Member State has no embassy or consulate able to help, is entitled to the protection of the diplomatic or consular authorities of any other Member State, on the same conditions as that state's own nationals. The assistance covers situations such as arrest, being the victim of a crime, serious accident or illness, and death, as well as emergency repatriation. The assisting state may ask the citizen's own Member State to reimburse the costs it incurs, and the citizen can in turn be asked to repay them. The right applies to unrepresented citizens: a traveller whose own country has an embassy able to help must turn to it.

## v05-11-c

The European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights, based in Vienna, was established in 2007. ## v05-12-c

When the European Commission considers that a Member State has failed to fulfil an obligation under EU law, it can open an infringement procedure under Article 258 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union. It first sends a letter of formal notice and, if the reply is unsatisfactory, a reasoned opinion setting a deadline for compliance. Only if the Member State still does not comply may the Commission refer the case to the Court of Justice. Most cases are closed long before that stage.

If the Member State then fails to comply with the Court's judgment, the Commission can take the case back to the Court, which under Article 260 may impose a lump sum, a periodic penalty payment, or both. Since the Treaty of Lisbon, however, where a Member State has failed to notify measures transposing a directive, the Commission may ask the Court to impose financial penalties in the first judgment. Individuals who complain to the Commission cannot compel it to act.

## v05-13-c

Article 7 of the Treaty on European Union deals with Member States that threaten the Union's founding values. Under its preventive arm, the Council, acting by a four-fifths majority with the consent of the European Parliament, may determine that there is a clear risk of a serious breach of those values. Such a proposal may come from one third of the Member States, from the Parliament or from the Commission.

Under the sanctioning arm, the European Council, acting unanimously on a proposal from one third of the Member States or from the Commission, and again with the Parliament's consent, may determine the existence of a serious and persistent breach. Only after such a determination may the Council suspend certain of that state's rights, including its voting rights. The preventive arm has been triggered twice: by the Commission against Poland in 2017 and by the Parliament against Hungary in 2018. Many observers attribute the procedure's limited impact to the unanimity required at the second stage.

## v05-14-c

Since 1 January 2021, the EU has had a general regime of conditionality to protect its budget. Under the regulation, the Council can adopt measures such as suspending payments or commitments when breaches of the principles of the rule of law in a Member State affect, or seriously risk affecting, the sound financial management of the EU budget or the Union's financial interests in a sufficiently direct way. ## v05-15-c

Mutual recognition, which EU leaders at the Tampere European Council in 1999 called the cornerstone of judicial cooperation, means that a decision taken by a judicial authority in one Member State is recognised and executed in another with a minimum of formalities, rather than being re-examined from scratch. It rests on mutual trust that each national justice system offers adequate safeguards.

The European Investigation Order applies this principle to evidence. Since 2017, an authority investigating a crime in one of the participating Member States can ask another to carry out investigative measures, such as hearing witnesses, searching premises or obtaining banking information, and to transfer the evidence obtained. The executing authority must decide whether to recognise the order within 30 days and, in principle, carry out the measure within 90 days of that decision. It may refuse only on grounds listed in the directive, and it may in some cases use a different, less intrusive measure if that would achieve the same result.

## v05-16-c

Between 2010 and 2016, the EU adopted six directives setting common minimum standards for the rights of suspects and accused persons in criminal proceedings. ## v05-17-c

EU anti-discrimination law rests largely on two directives adopted in 2000. The Racial Equality Directive prohibits discrimination on grounds of racial or ethnic origin not only in employment but also in social protection, education and access to goods and services available to the public, including housing. A Commission proposal of 2008 to extend protection beyond employment for these other grounds did not obtain the unanimity required in the Council. ## v05-18-c

In The Logic of Scientific Discovery, first published in German in 1934, Karl Popper proposed a criterion for distinguishing science from non-science. A theory is scientific, he argued, not because it has been confirmed many times but because it is falsifiable: it rules out certain observable outcomes and could therefore be shown to be false. No finite number of observations can prove a universal statement such as 'all swans are white', whereas a single black swan refutes it. Popper used the criterion to criticise theories that he believed could accommodate any outcome, citing certain versions of psychoanalysis and of Marxist history. A theory that explains everything, he argued, explains nothing.

Critics pointed to complications. Every prediction also relies on auxiliary assumptions about instruments and background theories, so when a prediction fails, one of these assumptions can be blamed rather than the main theory. Thomas Kuhn added that scientists often keep a successful theory despite anomalies, and that this tenacity has sometimes been productive.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. For Popper, a theory compatible with every possible observation would not be scientific.
B. Popper devised his criterion mainly to refute psychoanalysis and Marxist history.
C. Critics showed that testing a theory against observation is logically impossible.
D. Kuhn argued that keeping a theory despite anomalies has always proved productive.

## v05-19-c

In 2015, a large collaboration of researchers reported an attempt to repeat 100 experiments published in three leading psychology journals. Whereas 97 of the original studies had reported statistically significant results, only about 36% of the replications did. The project did not show that the original findings were wrong: a single failed replication can itself be a false negative, and some replications differed from the originals in their samples or procedures.

The results nevertheless fuelled a debate about a 'replication crisis'. Proposed causes include journals' preference for novel and positive findings, small samples, and p-hacking, in which researchers try several analyses and report the one that crosses the threshold of significance. Reforms have followed. In preregistration, researchers publicly record their hypotheses and analysis plan before collecting data. Some journals now offer 'registered reports', giving in-principle acceptance based on the study design before results are known, with final publication subject to faithful execution and quality checks. Supporters argue that these measures reduce bias.

B. Positive results cannot sway the initial acceptance of a registered report.

## v05-20-c

Magna Carta was agreed by King John of England at Runnymede in June 1215, under pressure from rebellious barons. It was a practical peace settlement rather than a statement of universal principles: most of its clauses dealt with specific grievances about feudal payments, inheritance, royal forests and the administration of justice. It failed almost at once, and civil war resumed.

The charter nevertheless survived. After John's death in 1216, the regents governing for his young son Henry III reissued it in revised form, and further versions followed in 1217 and 1225. The 1225 version became the definitive text. Its lasting fame rests above all on the promise that no free man would be imprisoned or deprived of his property except by the lawful judgement of his peers or by the law of the land. In the seventeenth century, opponents of royal power reinterpreted this promise as a guarantee of fundamental liberties, and the charter later influenced constitutional thinking in the American colonies.

## v05-21-c

In The Rules of Sociological Method, published in 1895, Émile Durkheim made a claim that many readers found shocking: crime is a normal feature of society. His point was partly statistical: crime is found in societies of every type, so a society entirely without it would be the abnormal case. Crime also performs functions. When an offender is punished, members of the community are reminded of the norms they share. Crime may also prepare change: by showing that existing norms can be challenged, it leaves room for moral evolution, as Socrates, condemned under Athenian law, showed.

Durkheim illustrated the inevitability of deviance with an imaginary community of saints in an exemplary monastery. There, he suggested, crimes in the ordinary sense would be unknown, but faults that seem venial to ordinary people would cause the same scandal that ordinary offences cause elsewhere. What counts as crime, in other words, depends on the strength of collective sentiments, not on a fixed list of acts.

B. Durkheim based his claim mainly on crime statistics he had collected in France.
C. Most sociologists of Durkheim's own time rejected his theory of crime.
D. Durkheim argued that every crime contributes to the moral evolution of its society.

## v05-22-c

In A Theory of Justice, published in 1971, John Rawls asked which principles of justice people would choose if they did not know what position they would occupy in society. Behind this 'veil of ignorance', no one can tailor the principles to their own advantage. Rawls argued that they would choose two principles. The first gives each person an equal right to the most extensive system of basic liberties compatible with a similar system for all. The second allows social and economic inequalities only if they are attached to positions open to all and are to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged members of society, a condition known as the difference principle.

Robert Nozick objected that the theory fails to respect people's rights over the fruits of their work. Others have questioned whether the parties would really be as cautious as Rawls assumed, since a willingness to gamble could lead them to prefer arrangements that maximise average welfare.
