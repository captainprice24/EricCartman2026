## v08-01-c

The European Pillar of Social Rights was proclaimed jointly by the European Parliament, the Council and the Commission at a summit in Gothenburg in November 2017. It sets out 20 principles in three chapters, ranging from the right to education and training to the right to a minimum income and to affordable long-term care.

The proclamation itself does not create new legal obligations. Its principles are put into practice through EU legislation, funding and the coordination of national policies, and many of the areas concerned remain largely the responsibility of Member States and the social partners. In 2021 the Commission presented an action plan with three headline targets for 2030: at least 78% of people aged 20 to 64 in employment, at least 60% of adults in training every year, and at least 15 million fewer people at risk of poverty or social exclusion. ## v08-02-c

The Directive on adequate minimum wages, adopted in 2022, does not set a common minimum wage for the EU, nor does it oblige any country to introduce a statutory minimum wage. In most Member States a minimum wage is fixed by law, but in five, including Denmark, Sweden and Italy, minimum pay is set through collective agreements, and these countries may keep that model.

Countries with a statutory minimum wage must have clear procedures for setting and updating it, and to assess its adequacy they may use indicative reference values such as 60% of the gross median wage. A separate provision applies to every Member State, whatever its wage-setting model: where fewer than 80% of workers are covered by collective agreements, the government must draw up an action plan to promote collective bargaining. On a challenge brought by Denmark, the Court of Justice in November 2025 annulled two specific provisions but left the rest in force, including the rules on collective bargaining.

## v08-03-c

The Pay Transparency Directive, adopted in 2023, aims to enforce equal pay for men and women for equal work or work of equal value. Several of its rights apply to all employers, whatever their size. Job applicants must be told the starting pay, or its range, early enough for informed negotiation, and employers may no longer ask candidates about their previous pay. Workers may request information on average pay, broken down by sex, for colleagues doing work of equal value.

Reporting duties, by contrast, depend on size. Employers with at least 250 workers must report on their gender pay gap every year, and those with 100 to 249 workers every three years. Smaller employers have no such duty under the Directive, though Member States may impose one. Where reporting reveals a gap of at least 5% in any category of workers that the employer cannot justify on objective, gender-neutral grounds and does not correct within six months, a joint pay assessment with workers' representatives is required.

## v08-04-c

The Work-Life Balance Directive, applicable since August 2022, sets minimum standards for leave and flexible working. Fathers, or equivalent second parents, are entitled to at least ten working days of paternity leave around the birth, paid at least at the level of national sick pay. The right to the leave itself cannot depend on how long the worker has been employed, but Member States may make the payment conditional on up to six months of prior employment.

Each parent is also entitled to four months of parental leave, two of which cannot be transferred and must be paid. Workers caring for a relative with a serious medical need are entitled to five working days of carers' leave per year; Member States decide whether this leave is paid. Parents of children up to at least eight years old and carers may request flexible arrangements, such as reduced hours. Employers must respond within a reasonable period and give reasons for any refusal.

## v08-05-c

The Platform Work Directive, adopted in 2024, is the first piece of EU law to regulate the use of algorithms in managing people at work, and Member States have until December 2026 to transpose it. In 2021 the Commission estimated that more than 28 million people in the EU worked through digital labour platforms, in activities ranging from ride-hailing and food delivery to translation, and that many may have been wrongly classified as self-employed.

The Directive therefore introduces a legal presumption of employment. Where facts indicating control and direction are found, as determined under national law and collective agreements, the person is presumed to be in an employment relationship with the platform. The presumption can be rebutted, but it is then for the platform to prove that the relationship is not one of employment. The Directive does not automatically reclassify anyone. Decisions that significantly affect a person, such as suspending an account, must be taken by a human being.

## v08-06-c

## v08-07-c

The Youth Guarantee was launched in 2013, when youth unemployment in the EU had risen sharply after the financial and economic crisis. ## v08-08-c

EU rules on social security coordination, set out mainly in Regulation 883/2004, do not replace national systems with a single European one. Each country decides who is insured and which benefits are granted; the EU rules determine which country's system applies in cross-border situations. They apply in the EU Member States and in Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland.

A basic principle is that a person is covered by the legislation of one country at a time. As a rule, this is the country where the person works, even if he or she lives elsewhere. People who normally work in two or more countries are insured where they live if they do at least 25% of their work there. Under the aggregation principle, periods of insurance completed in one country count when another country assesses entitlement to a benefit. Jobseekers who move abroad may keep their unemployment benefit for three months, which the employment services can extend to a maximum of six.

## v08-09-c

The European Medicines Agency (EMA) evaluates medicines for human and veterinary use. Its scientific committees are made up largely of experts from national medicines authorities, which share the work of assessing and monitoring medicines with the Agency and the Commission.

Under the centralised procedure, a company submits a single application to EMA, whose Committee for Medicinal Products for Human Use assesses the medicine and gives an opinion on whether it should be authorised. The legally binding decision is then taken by the European Commission, and the authorisation is valid in all EU Member States and in Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway. The procedure is compulsory for certain categories, such as biotechnology medicines, medicines for rare diseases and medicines with a new active substance to treat cancer, diabetes or HIV/AIDS. Most generic and non-prescription medicines, by contrast, are authorised by national authorities. Authorisation does not make a medicine available to patients everywhere on the same terms: each Member State decides on its price and on whether its health system pays for it.

## v08-10-c

The European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC) was established in 2004 and began operating the following year in Solna, near Stockholm. Its task is to identify, assess and communicate threats to human health from communicable diseases. It collects surveillance data from Member States, publishes rapid risk assessments when outbreaks occur and advises national authorities and the EU institutions.

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed the limits of this model, and in 2022 the Centre's mandate was strengthened. It now makes recommendations on preparedness and can deploy experts through an EU Health Task Force to support Member States and, at their request, countries outside the EU. Even so, the ECDC has no regulatory powers. It cannot impose public health measures such as travel restrictions, school closures or vaccination requirements; such decisions remain with national governments. Its influence therefore depends largely on the quality of its scientific work and on the readiness of national authorities to act on its advice.

## v08-11-c

The Directive on patients' rights in cross-border healthcare, adopted in 2011, allows people insured in one EU country to receive healthcare in another and to be reimbursed by their home system. Reimbursement is limited to the amount that the home system would have paid had the same treatment been provided at home, and it may not exceed the actual cost.

Member States may require prior authorisation for certain types of care, notably treatment involving at least one overnight stay in hospital. Authorisation may not be refused, however, where the treatment is among the benefits to which the patient is entitled at home and cannot be provided there within a medically justifiable time limit. Each country must have at least one national contact point that informs patients about their rights and about the providers available. The Directive also laid the basis for European Reference Networks, which link specialist centres so that expertise on rare and complex diseases, rather than the patients themselves, can travel.

## v08-12-c

The Tobacco Products Directive, which has applied since May 2016, governs how tobacco and related products are manufactured, presented and sold in the EU. Packs of cigarettes and roll-your-own tobacco must carry combined health warnings, consisting of a picture, a text warning and information on quitting, which cover 65% of both the front and the back of the pack. The Directive does not deal with tobacco taxation, which is governed by separate EU rules on excise duties. In 2016 the Court of Justice rejected challenges to several of its provisions, including the ban on menthol.

The European Heritage Label is awarded to sites that have played a significant role in the history, culture or integration of Europe. Monuments, archaeological sites, landscapes, museums and archives can all apply, as can places associated with intangible heritage. Applicants must submit a project explaining how they will promote the European dimension of the site, in particular through educational activities for young people. A site can lose the label if it no longer meets the criteria.

## v08-15-c

The Bologna Process began in 1999, when ministers responsible for higher education from 29 European countries signed a declaration in Bologna committing them to make their degree systems more comparable. It is often mistaken for an EU policy, but it is an intergovernmental process: its membership extends to nearly fifty countries, and its commitments are agreed by ministers rather than adopted as EU law. The European Commission is a member and supports the process with funding, but it does not direct it.

In 2010 the participating countries launched the European Higher Education Area. Countries that are slow to carry out the reforms face no legal sanctions, though their record is made public.

## v08-16-c

In 1935 the American psychologist John Ridley Stroop published a study that became one of the most frequently replicated findings in experimental psychology. He asked participants to name the colour of the ink in which words were printed. When the words were names of other colours, such as the word 'red' printed in blue ink, people took considerably longer to name the ink colour than when they named the colours of plain squares. Reading the words aloud, by contrast, was hardly slowed by a mismatched ink colour.

The standard explanation is that, for literate adults, reading is a highly practised and largely automatic process, whereas naming colours requires deliberate attention. When the two conflict, the automatic response has to be suppressed, which takes time and sometimes fails. Extended practice at naming colours tends to reduce the interference without removing it. The task is now widely used to study attention and self-control, although the size of the effect varies with practice, language and the design of the task.

B. Stroop himself concluded that reading is automatic for adults who can read.
C. Naming the ink was slower when the word named a different colour.
D. Practice at naming colours reduces the interference more in children than in adults.

## v08-17-c

In the 1840s the Vienna General Hospital had two maternity clinics. Women were admitted to them on alternate days, yet mortality from puerperal fever, an infection after childbirth, was much higher in the first clinic, where doctors and medical students worked, than in the second, which was staffed by midwives. In 1847 Ignaz Semmelweis, an assistant in the first clinic, concluded that 'cadaverous particles' carried on the hands of doctors and students coming from the dissecting room were causing the disease.

He required staff to wash their hands in a chlorinated lime solution before examining patients, and mortality in the first clinic fell sharply, to a level close to that of the second. Many of his colleagues nonetheless rejected his conclusions, which conflicted with the prevailing view that the disease had many causes. Recognition came later, once Louis Pasteur and Joseph Lister had established that microorganisms cause infection. Semmelweis had found a way of preventing the disease without knowing what the causal agent actually was.

## v08-18-c

In the mid-nineteenth century most doctors believed that cholera was spread by 'miasma', a poisonous vapour. The London physician John Snow doubted this: because the disease attacked the digestive system first, he argued, it must be caused by something swallowed rather than inhaled.

During an outbreak in Soho in 1854, Snow mapped the homes of those who died and found that they clustered around a public water pump in Broad Street. Workers at a nearby brewery, who were given beer and had their own well, were largely spared, while some victims living further away turned out to have preferred the Broad Street water. On his advice the pump handle was removed. New cases had, however, already been falling, partly because many residents had fled, so the episode alone did not prove his theory. Stronger evidence came from comparing households supplied by two water companies, one drawing water from a sewage-contaminated stretch of the Thames, the other from upstream. Death rates were far higher among customers of the first.

## v08-19-c

The idea that the language people speak shapes the way they think is often called the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis, after the linguist Edward Sapir and his student Benjamin Lee Whorf. In its strong form, known as linguistic determinism, it holds that language sets the limits of thought, so that speakers cannot form concepts for which their language has no words. Most linguists and psychologists now reject this version.

A weaker claim, that language influences certain kinds of thinking, has found some experimental support. Russian, for example, has separate basic terms for lighter and darker blues. In one study, Russian speakers told two shades of blue apart more quickly when they fell on different sides of the Russian boundary than when they fell on the same side, while English speakers showed no such difference. The advantage disappeared when the Russian speakers had to perform a verbal task at the same time. Even supporters of the weaker claim stress that such effects are usually small and depend on the task.

A. The Russian speakers' advantage appears to depend on language being available at the time.
B. Whorf developed his ideas about language and thought by studying Russian colour terms.
C. Russian speakers would probably show a similar advantage with shades of green.
D. Experiments show that language influences all kinds of thinking without limiting it.

## v08-20-c

In 1786 William Jones, a British judge in India, observed that Sanskrit bore so strong a resemblance to Greek and Latin, in verb roots and grammatical forms, that the three must have sprung from a common source. He was not the first to notice such similarities, but his remark is often taken as the starting point of comparative linguistics. The family descended from that source, Indo-European, includes most languages of Europe, among them the Germanic, Romance and Slavic languages, as well as Persian and many languages of northern India.

Linguists reconstruct the parent language, Proto-Indo-European, by working out regular sound correspondences between its descendants. Latin pater and Sanskrit pitar, for example, correspond to English father, reflecting a systematic change of p to f in the Germanic languages. Similarities that do not follow regular correspondences are treated with suspicion, because they may result from borrowing or chance. No texts in Proto-Indo-European survive, so reconstructed forms are hypotheses. Several European languages, including Basque, Finnish and Hungarian, do not belong to the family.

B. Jones learned Sanskrit in India in order to study the country's ancient legal texts.
C. A Hungarian–German resemblance lacking regular sound correspondences would be suspect.
D. Sound correspondences let linguists rebuild Proto-Indo-European exactly as it was spoken.

## v08-21-c

The demographic transition model describes how populations change as societies develop. In the first stage, both birth and death rates are high and the population grows slowly, if at all. In the second, death rates fall, thanks mainly to a better food supply, sanitation and medicine, while birth rates remain high, so the population grows rapidly. In the third stage birth rates also decline, as more children survive, education spreads and families choose to have fewer children, and growth slows. In the fourth, both rates are low and the population is roughly stable.

The model was developed from the experience of Western Europe, and it describes rather than explains. Some demographers add a fifth stage for countries where fertility has fallen well below the replacement level, which in countries with low mortality is about 2.1 children per woman. In such countries deaths may eventually exceed births, and the population may decline unless immigration makes up the difference.

B. In the model, rapid growth occurs because mortality falls before fertility does.
C. Countries with fertility below 2.1 children per woman always see their population shrink.
D. In the third stage, birth rates fall solely because more children now survive.
