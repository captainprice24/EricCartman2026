## v06-01-c

Article 107 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union declares state support that favours certain undertakings incompatible with the internal market where it distorts competition and affects trade between Member States. The prohibition is not absolute: the Treaty lists categories that are or may be compatible, such as aid to remedy a serious disturbance in the economy of a Member State.

As a rule, governments must notify planned aid to the European Commission and may not grant it until the Commission has approved it. Many measures, however, escape this obligation. The General Block Exemption Regulation allows some aid for research, training or environmental protection to be granted without prior notification, provided that its conditions are met, and small amounts below the de minimis ceiling fall outside the rules altogether. Aid paid without the required approval is unlawful. If the Commission then finds it incompatible, it orders recovery from the beneficiary, with interest. The aim is to restore the earlier situation, not to punish the company.

## v06-02-c

Under the EU Merger Regulation, concentrations with an 'EU dimension' must be notified to the European Commission and may not be completed until they have been cleared. In the main test, the companies concerned must have a combined worldwide turnover above €5 billion, and at least two of them must each have EU turnover above €250 million. Even then, the deal is left to national authorities if each company achieves more than two-thirds of its EU turnover in one and the same Member State.

The Commission assesses whether a deal would significantly impede effective competition, in particular by creating or strengthening a dominant position. Most notified mergers are cleared in a first phase of 25 working days; where serious doubts remain, an in-depth second phase normally lasts 90 working days. Outright prohibitions are rare. More often, the parties offer remedies, such as selling a business or licensing technology, and the deal is approved subject to those commitments. Critics argue that the rules can prevent the creation of European champions.

## v06-03-c

Article 101 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union prohibits agreements that restrict competition, and cartels, in which competitors secretly fix prices or share markets, are among the most serious. The Commission can fine a company up to 10% of its worldwide turnover. Within that ceiling, the amount depends on the sales concerned and the gravity and duration of the infringement, and it can be increased for repeat offenders.

Because cartels are secret, the Commission relies on leniency. The first company to report a cartel with evidence enabling an inspection or a finding of infringement may receive full immunity from fines. Later applicants that supply evidence of significant added value obtain reductions: 30 to 50% for the first of them, 20 to 30% for the next and up to 20% for any others. Leniency does not protect against everything. Victims can sue cartel members for damages before national courts, although an immune firm is generally liable only towards its own direct and indirect purchasers or providers.

## v06-04-c

The Digital Markets Act, applicable since May 2023, targets large online platforms that act as 'gatekeepers'. A company is presumed to be a gatekeeper if it provides a core platform service, such as a search engine or an app store, in at least three Member States and meets two tests of size and reach. It must have an EU turnover of at least €7.5 billion in each of the last three years or a market value of at least €75 billion, and it must have at least 45 million monthly active end users and 10,000 yearly active business users in the EU. The Commission designates gatekeepers and decides on any attempt to rebut the presumption.

Unlike traditional competition law, which acts once harm has been shown, the Act sets obligations in advance. Gatekeepers may not rank their own products more favourably than those of rivals. Breaches can lead to fines of up to 10% of worldwide turnover, or 20% for repeated infringements. Six gatekeepers were designated in September 2023.

## v06-05-c

Value added tax (VAT) is charged at each stage of production and distribution, but businesses can deduct the VAT they pay on their purchases from the VAT they charge on their sales. The tax is thus in principle borne by the final consumer, while businesses act as collectors for the state. The EU's VAT Directive sets common rules, yet Member States choose their own rates within limits: the standard rate must be at least 15%. A reform adopted in 2022 gave governments more flexibility, including a zero rate for a limited number of essential items.

For online sales to consumers in other Member States, VAT is generally due where the goods are delivered. Since July 2021, sellers can declare and pay it through a single online portal, the One-Stop Shop, in their own country instead of registering in each customer's Member State. The gap between the VAT due and the VAT collected remains large, partly because of fraud exploiting the exemption for goods moving between Member States.

## v06-06-c

The Services Directive, adopted in 2006, was designed to make it easier for businesses to set up in another Member State or to provide services there temporarily. Services account for most of the EU's economic activity, yet cross-border trade in services has long lagged behind trade in goods, partly because of national rules on authorisations, legal form and professional qualifications.

The Directive requires Member States to simplify procedures and to provide 'points of single contact', through which providers can complete formalities online. Any requirement imposed on a provider from another Member State offering services temporarily must be non-discriminatory, justified by reasons such as public policy or public health, and proportionate. Certain requirements, such as conditions based on nationality, are banned outright. Several sectors, including financial services, healthcare and transport, are excluded. The original proposal would have subjected cross-border providers mainly to the rules of their home country, but it met strong opposition over fears of 'social dumping', and the final text dropped that principle.

## v06-07-c

A posted worker is an employee sent by an employer to carry out a service in another Member State on a temporary basis. The Posting of Workers Directive guarantees posted workers a core set of the host country's terms and conditions, including maximum work periods, minimum paid annual leave and health and safety rules. Since a revision took effect in July 2020, they are also entitled to the host country's remuneration as set by law or by universally applicable collective agreements, not merely its minimum wage.

When a posting lasts more than 12 months, almost all of the host country's labour law applies, with exceptions such as the rules on concluding and terminating contracts and supplementary occupational pension schemes. This period can be extended to 18 months if the employer submits a motivated notification. Social security follows separate rules. Under the EU coordination regulations, a posted worker generally remains covered by the sending country's system for up to 24 months, as shown by a certificate known as the A1 form.

## v06-08-c

In the late 1970s a German retail group wanted to import Cassis de Dijon, a French blackcurrant liqueur, but the German authorities refused because its alcohol content, between 15% and 20%, was below the 25% minimum that German law required for fruit liqueurs. A product lawfully produced and marketed in one Member State should in principle be admitted to the markets of the others, even if it does not meet their technical rules exactly.

The German rule, however, was not necessary: consumers could be informed of the alcohol content by a label, a less restrictive means of reaching the same goal. The ruling established the principle of mutual recognition and led the Commission to focus harmonisation on essential requirements.

## v06-09-c

The CE marking appears on many products sold in the European Economic Area, from toys and electrical appliances to machinery and medical devices. By affixing it, the manufacturer declares that the product meets the EU requirements on safety, health and environmental protection that apply to it. The marking is not a sign of quality, nor does it show that a product was made in Europe: goods manufactured in China or the United States must carry it too if they fall within the scope of the relevant legislation.

How conformity is checked depends on the risks involved. For many low-risk products, the manufacturer can carry out the assessment itself. For products such as certain machinery or higher-risk medical devices, an independent 'notified body' must be involved. Products not covered by any legislation requiring CE marking must not carry it. National authorities check products already on sale and can order their withdrawal. Online marketplaces have made enforcement harder, because unsafe goods are often shipped directly to consumers from outside the EU.

## v06-10-c

The EU defines small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in a Commission recommendation of 2003, and the definition determines eligibility for many EU funding programmes. A medium-sized enterprise has fewer than 250 employees and either an annual turnover of no more than €50 million or a balance sheet total of no more than €43 million. A small enterprise has fewer than 50 employees and a turnover or balance sheet total of no more than €10 million.

The staff ceiling must always be respected, but a firm may choose which of the two financial ceilings to meet. This option exists because firms in trade and distribution typically have high turnover relative to their assets. Independence also matters. A company that is part of a larger group cannot count only its own figures: the data of linked enterprises, and a proportion of those of partner enterprises, are added to its own. Otherwise, a large group could split itself into small companies to qualify for support.

## v06-12-c

The European Semester, introduced in 2011, is the annual cycle through which the EU coordinates the economic, fiscal and employment policies of its Member States. It starts in the autumn, when the Commission publishes its assessment of the economic situation and, for euro area countries, its opinions on their draft budgetary plans. In the spring, the Commission analyses each country and proposes country-specific recommendations, which are endorsed by the European Council in June and formally adopted by the Council in July.

The recommendations cover areas such as public finances, pensions, labour markets and the business environment. They are not legally binding as such, and governments decide how far to follow them. The Commission's own assessments regularly find that only a minority are fully or substantially implemented within a year, though progress tends to be greater over several years. Every year, public authorities in the EU spend around 14% of the Union's GDP on works, supplies and services, from roads and hospitals to school meals. ## v06-14-c

The Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices (HICP) measures changes over time in the prices of goods and services bought by households. Each Member State's statistical office compiles its own index using common methods set in EU law, and Eurostat, the EU's statistical office, aggregates the results for the euro area and the EU. Because the methods are harmonised, the figures can be compared across countries, which national consumer price indices, built on different national conventions, do not always allow.

The index is based on a basket reflecting what households actually spend their money on. The weights are updated every year, so a category on which households spend more counts for more in the following year's index. Weights also differ between countries: food accounts for a larger share of spending in some Member States than in others. Eurostat publishes a flash estimate for the euro area at the end of each month or shortly afterwards, before full results become available around the middle of the following month.

## v06-17-c

Article 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union prohibits the abuse of a dominant position. Being dominant is not unlawful in itself: a company may win a large market share by offering better or cheaper products, and competition law does not aim to protect less efficient rivals from that. What is prohibited is conduct by which a dominant firm exploits customers or excludes competitors by means other than competition on the merits, such as charging unfair prices or tying one product to another. A market share above 50% is generally treated as a strong indication of dominance, although lower shares can be enough in some circumstances.

In 2017 the Commission fined Google €2.42 billion for favouring its own comparison shopping service in its general search results while demoting rival services. Google challenged the decision, but in 2024 the Court of Justice upheld it, confirming that a dominant platform can abuse its position by treating its own services more favourably than those of competitors.

## v06-18-c

In 1970 the economist George Akerlof published an article on the market for used cars, which American slang calls 'lemons' when they turn out to be defective. Several leading journals had rejected it. Akerlof's point was that sellers know far more about the quality of their cars than buyers do. Unable to tell good cars from bad ones, buyers will pay only a price that reflects average quality. Owners of good cars find that price too low and keep them off the market, which lowers the average quality on offer, and with it the price. In the extreme case, the market for good used cars disappears altogether.

The same logic of 'asymmetric information' applies in insurance: people who know they are at high risk are the most eager to buy cover, so premiums set for the average customer may drive low-risk customers away. Markets have developed ways to reduce the problem, such as warranties, brand reputations and independent inspections, and governments sometimes make insurance compulsory.

B. Once buyers cannot judge used car quality, the market for good cars always disappears.
C. Warranties and brand reputations have solved asymmetric information in used cars.
D. Making insurance compulsory can keep low-risk customers from leaving the market.

## v06-19-c

In July 1944, delegates from 44 Allied nations met at Bretton Woods in New Hampshire to design a monetary system for the post-war world, hoping to avoid a return to the competitive devaluations and trade barriers of the 1930s. The system fixed each currency to the US dollar within narrow margins, while the dollar itself was convertible into gold at $35 an ounce for foreign central banks. Exchange rates could be changed only to correct a 'fundamental disequilibrium', and the new International Monetary Fund was to lend to countries facing temporary balance-of-payments difficulties.

The system worked for about a quarter of a century, but it contained a weakness. As other countries accumulated dollars, the amount held abroad came to exceed US gold reserves, raising doubts about whether convertibility could be kept. In August 1971 President Richard Nixon suspended the convertibility of the dollar into gold. Attempts to restore fixed rates soon failed, and by 1973 the major currencies were floating. The IMF survived the collapse and took on new roles.

## v06-20-c

Standard economic theory assumes that people choose the option that suits them best, however the choice is presented. Behavioural economists have shown that presentation matters, and one of the clearest examples is the power of defaults: the option that applies if a person does nothing.

Studies of US firms around 2000 found that when pension schemes switched from active sign-up to automatic enrolment, with the possibility to opt out, participation among new employees rose sharply, although the financial incentives were unchanged. Researchers point to inertia, the effort of comparing options and the sense that the default carries the employer's implicit recommendation. The studies also found a less welcome effect: many workers stayed at the default contribution rate and investment fund even when these were lower or more cautious than the ones employees had typically chosen before. Since 2012 the United Kingdom has gradually required employers to enrol eligible workers automatically in a workplace pension, while allowing them to opt out. Critics warn that defaults can be manipulative.

## v06-21-c

In a widely cited article published in 1968, the ecologist Garrett Hardin described the 'tragedy of the commons'. Imagine a pasture open to all herders. Each herder gains the full benefit of adding another animal, while the cost of overgrazing is shared by everyone, so each keeps adding animals until the pasture is ruined. Hardin concluded that shared resources would be destroyed unless they were either privatised or controlled by the state.

The political scientist Elinor Ostrom challenged this conclusion. Studying fisheries, forests, irrigation systems and mountain pastures around the world, she found that users were often able to manage a common resource sustainably without either privatisation or central control. Successful arrangements tended to share certain features: clear boundaries on who may use the resource, rules adapted to local conditions, monitoring by people accountable to the users, graduated sanctions for rule-breakers and cheap ways of resolving conflicts. Ostrom did not claim that communities always succeed; she documented many failures too, particularly where users could not communicate.

A. A fishing village with local catch rules and escalating fines could avoid Hardin's tragedy.
B. Ostrom found that common resources can be managed sustainably only under state control.
C. Hardin later accepted that his conclusion about shared resources was too pessimistic.
D. Communities that manage a common resource themselves always outperform the state.

## v06-22-c

The term 'Dutch disease' was coined by The Economist magazine in 1977 to describe the problems that followed the discovery of a large natural gas field in the Netherlands in 1959. As gas exports grew, the Dutch currency strengthened, and manufacturing firms found it harder to sell their goods abroad. Unemployment rose even though the country was earning substantial revenue from gas.

Economists have since used the term for a wider pattern. When a country starts exporting large amounts of a natural resource, the inflow of foreign currency tends to push up its exchange rate. Other export sectors, such as manufacturing or agriculture, become less competitive, while labour and capital may move towards the resource sector and towards services that cannot be traded across borders, such as construction. The effect is not inevitable. Norway places its oil and gas revenue in a sovereign wealth fund that invests abroad, and the government limits how much of the fund it spends each year.

A. Every sector of an economy loses competitiveness when a resource boom lifts the currency.
B. The Netherlands cut its gas output in the 1980s to protect its manufacturing sector.
C. Norway's sovereign wealth fund invests mainly in shares of large technology companies.
D. In the Netherlands, rising gas revenue went together with rising unemployment.
