## v04-01-c

Under the Treaties, the common commercial policy is an exclusive competence of the European Union: in principle, only the Union may conclude binding agreements on trade in goods and services and on foreign direct investment. The Commission negotiates trade agreements; the Council then decides, usually by qualified majority, and the Parliament must give its consent.

For many years, however, agreements were concluded as 'mixed' agreements, signed by both the Union and its Member States, because they also covered matters of shared competence. A mixed agreement must be ratified by every Member State, so its full entry into force can take many years. In 2017 the Court of Justice, asked for an opinion on the agreement negotiated with Singapore, found that most of the deal fell within exclusive competence, but not the rules on portfolio investment and investor-state dispute settlement. The Union then began to split such deals in two: a trade agreement that it can conclude alone, and a separate investment protection agreement that remains mixed.

B. After a split, the trade agreement needs no ratification by each Member State.
C. The Court's opinion on the Singapore agreement was requested by the Commission.
D. The Council always decides by qualified majority when it concludes a trade deal.

## v04-02-c

Turkey has formed a customs union with the European Union since the end of 1995. Industrial goods and processed agricultural products move between the two sides without customs duties, and Turkey applies the Union's common external tariff to imports of these goods from third countries. Services, public procurement and most unprocessed farm products are not covered.

Turkish officials have long criticised an asymmetry in this arrangement. When the Union concludes a free trade agreement, its partner lowers its duties on EU goods, while the partner's goods entering the EU at preferential rates can, in principle, then circulate freely into Turkey. Yet the partner has no obligation to open its own market to Turkish goods, because Turkey is not a party to the agreement and must negotiate a separate one. In 2016 the Commission proposed modernising the customs union and extending it to services and procurement, but the Council did not give it a negotiating mandate, mainly for reasons linked to the wider political relationship.

## v04-03-c

Dumping occurs when a company exports a product at a price below its 'normal value', which is generally the price charged on the exporter's home market. World Trade Organization rules allow an importing country to counter it with anti-dumping duties.

In the European Union, before imposing such duties, the Commission must establish four things: that the imports are dumped, that the EU industry has suffered material injury, that the dumped imports caused that injury, and that measures would not be against the interest of the Union as a whole, an assessment that also takes account of importers and users. Definitive duties normally apply for five years and can be extended following an expiry review. Under the 'lesser duty rule', the duty is set below the full dumping margin when a lower rate would remove the injury, although since 2018 the Union may set this rule aside, for example when raw material prices in the exporting country are distorted.

B. Definitive duties stay in force indefinitely unless the exporter raises its prices.
C. Anti-dumping duties are likely to raise the prices paid by EU consumers.
D. Since 2018, the lesser duty rule is always set aside when raw material prices are distorted.

## v04-04-c

The anti-coercion instrument, which entered into force at the end of 2023, is meant to deter third countries from using trade or investment measures to pressure the European Union or a Member State into making a particular policy choice. Such pressure may be formal, such as tariffs or import bans, or informal, such as sudden boycotts.

The Commission first examines a possible case. If it concludes that coercion is taking place, it proposes that the Council confirm this finding. The Council decides by qualified majority, so a single government cannot block the determination. The Union then tries to end the coercion through engagement, such as negotiation or mediation. Only if this fails may the Commission adopt response measures, which must not exceed the injury suffered by the Union. Supporters hope that the instrument's mere existence will make its use unnecessary. Critics fear that it could invite retaliation, or that governments may hesitate to confront a major trading partner.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Coercion covers any measure by a third country that affects its trade with the Union.
B. The instrument was probably adopted in response to Chinese pressure on Lithuania.
C. A lone government objecting to a finding of coercion cannot stop it in the Council.
D. Critics are certain that any use of the instrument will provoke retaliation.

## v04-05-c

The European External Action Service (EEAS), which began work in January 2011, is the European Union's diplomatic service. Created by the Treaty of Lisbon, it supports the High Representative for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy, who conducts the common foreign and security policy, chairs the Foreign Affairs Council and is also a Vice-President of the Commission. This 'double hat' was meant to bring together the intergovernmental side of foreign policy and the Union's external instruments, such as trade and development aid, which the Commission largely manages.

The EEAS is a functionally autonomous body, neither a Commission department nor part of the Council's secretariat. Its staff come from the Commission, the Council's General Secretariat and national foreign ministries, whose seconded diplomats are meant to make up at least a third of its staff at policy level. It runs more than 140 EU delegations, which represent the Union as a whole. They do not replace national embassies, but they increasingly coordinate the work of Member States' missions on the ground.

## v04-06-c

Decisions under the common foreign and security policy are, as a rule, taken unanimously by the Council or the European Council, which gives each Member State a veto. The Treaty softens this rule in two ways. First, abstention does not prevent a unanimous decision. A government that qualifies its abstention with a formal declaration, known as constructive abstention, is freed from the obligation to apply the decision, but must accept that it commits the Union and refrain from conflicting action. If the members abstaining in this way represent at least one third of the Member States comprising at least one third of the Union's population, the decision is not adopted.

Second, the Council acts by qualified majority in listed cases, for instance when implementing a strategy already agreed unanimously by the European Council, or when appointing a special representative. Decisions with military or defence implications, however, are always excluded from majority voting. Several governments want majority voting extended, but others, often smaller states, fear being outvoted.

B. A state that abstains constructively must still apply the decision on its territory.
C. Majority voting is likely to be extended to decisions on sanctions within a few years.
D. Constructive abstention by states with a third of the population stops a decision.

## v04-07-c

Restrictive measures, commonly called sanctions, are among the Union's main foreign policy tools. They can target governments, companies, groups or individuals, and include arms embargoes, travel bans, asset freezes and restrictions on trade in particular goods. Their stated aim is to change policy or conduct, not to punish, and they are meant to be targeted so as to limit their impact on the wider population.

Adoption involves two steps. The Council first adopts a decision under the common foreign and security policy, which requires unanimity. Where the measures fall within the Union's competences, for instance an asset freeze or a trade restriction, the Council then adopts a regulation by qualified majority. Travel bans, by contrast, are implemented by the Member States themselves, which also enforce the measures and set penalties. Listed persons can challenge their designation before the General Court, and the courts have annulled a number of listings because the Council could not show sufficient evidence for the reasons it had given.

## v04-08-c

In development cooperation, the European Union and its Member States share competence in a particular way: the Union can conduct its own policy and fund its own programmes, but this does not prevent the Member States from pursuing theirs. The Treaty states that the primary objective of Union development policy is the reduction and, in the long term, the eradication of poverty. It also requires the Union to take account of development objectives in those of its other policies that are likely to affect developing countries, a principle known as policy coherence for development.

For 2021–2027, much EU funding for partner countries flows through Global Europe, the Union's largest external instrument. It merged several earlier instruments and brought the European Development Fund, previously financed outside the budget by national contributions, into the EU budget. Together, the Union and its Member States are the world's largest provider of official development assistance, and they have committed to raising it collectively to 0.7% of gross national income by 2030.

## v04-09-c

The European Union, together with its Member States, is one of the world's leading donors of humanitarian aid. Within the Commission, this work is managed by the Directorate-General for European Civil Protection and Humanitarian Aid Operations, still widely known by its former acronym, ECHO. The Commission does not deliver most of the aid itself: it funds around 200 partner organisations, including United Nations agencies, the Red Cross and non-governmental organisations.

EU humanitarian aid is bound by four principles: humanity, neutrality, impartiality and independence. Impartiality means that aid is allocated solely on the basis of need, without discrimination between or within affected populations; independence means that humanitarian objectives must remain separate from political, economic or military ones. These principles can be difficult to apply. Agencies need the consent of those who control a territory to reach people in need, and armed groups or governments often impose conditions or obstruct access. Donors are also criticised for paying more attention to crises that attract media coverage than to protracted, 'forgotten' emergencies.

## v04-10-c

When a disaster overwhelms a country's capacity to respond, its government can ask for help through the EU Civil Protection Mechanism. Created in 2001, the Mechanism includes all Member States and several non-EU participating states, but requests for assistance may come from any country in the world, as well as from the United Nations and some other international organisations. Requests are received by the Emergency Response Coordination Centre in Brussels, which passes them on to the participating states. Each of them decides voluntarily whether to offer teams, equipment or expertise, and the Union can co-finance their transport.

In 2019 the Union added rescEU, a reserve that includes firefighting aircraft, medical stockpiles and equipment for chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear emergencies. These capacities are hosted by participating states but financed largely or entirely from the EU budget, and they are deployed as a last resort. The Mechanism has been activated for floods, wildfires, earthquakes and pandemics, as well as for the war in Ukraine.

## v04-11-c

The European Neighbourhood Policy was launched in 2004 to build closer relations with the countries to the Union's east and south without offering them membership. It covers sixteen partners: ten in North Africa and the Middle East, and six in Eastern Europe and the South Caucasus, which since 2009 have also formed the Eastern Partnership. It offers closer trade ties, financial support and cooperation in exchange for political and economic reforms. After the Arab uprisings of 2011, the Union restated this as a 'more for more' approach: partners that reform faster should receive more support. Critics argue that the leverage was limited, since membership was not on offer, and that the Union often gave priority to stability and migration control over reform.

The line between neighbourhood and enlargement has since blurred. Ukraine and Moldova were granted candidate status in 2022, and Georgia in 2023, so three eastern partners now have the membership perspective that the policy originally withheld. Belarus, by contrast, suspended its participation in 2021.

## v04-12-c

## v04-13-c

Under its common security and defence policy, the European Union deploys civilian missions and military operations abroad for tasks such as crisis management and the training of security forces. They are launched by a unanimous Council decision. The Union has no army of its own: personnel and equipment are provided by the Member States.

Funding follows a distinction made in the Treaty. The running costs of civilian missions are paid from the EU budget, whereas expenditure arising from operations with military or defence implications cannot be charged to it. Common costs of military operations, such as their headquarters, are therefore financed by contributions from the Member States, today through the European Peace Facility, while each country pays for the troops and equipment it sends. Most costs thus 'lie where they fall'. The naval operation Atalanta, launched off Somalia in 2008 to protect World Food Programme vessels and deter piracy, is one of the Union's longest-running military operations; another, Aspides, has protected merchant shipping in the Red Sea since 2024.

## v04-14-c

The European Peace Facility, set up in 2021, is an off-budget fund through which the European Union finances action in the field of security and defence. Because the Treaty prevents military expenditure from being charged to the EU budget, it is fed by annual contributions from the Member States. It replaced two earlier arrangements: one covering the common costs of EU military operations, and another supporting peace operations led by the African Union.

Its main novelty was to let the Union finance military equipment for the armed forces of partner countries, including lethal weapons. After Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, it was used to reimburse Member States for weapons and ammunition delivered to Ukraine. Assistance measures require unanimity in the Council. A Member State may abstain constructively: it then does not pay towards lethal equipment and contributes instead to other, non-lethal measures. Even so, a single government can delay or block new tranches, as happened repeatedly from 2023 onward.

## v04-16-c

Through its Generalised Scheme of Preferences, the European Union lowers or removes import duties on goods from developing countries without asking for reciprocal concessions. The scheme has three tiers. The standard arrangement reduces duties on about two thirds of tariff lines for low and lower-middle-income countries. GSP+ removes duties on the same lines for vulnerable countries that ratify and effectively implement 27 international conventions on human and labour rights, the environment and good governance. 'Everything But Arms' grants the least developed countries duty-free and quota-free access for all products except arms and ammunition.

Preferences can be withdrawn, wholly or in part, for serious and systematic violations of core human and labour rights; in 2020, for instance, the Union suspended part of Cambodia's preferences. A country leaves the standard arrangement and GSP+ once the World Bank has classified it as upper-middle-income for three consecutive years, whereas least developed countries keep Everything But Arms until the United Nations removes them from its list of such countries, after which a transition period applies.

## v04-17-c

Article 42(7) of the Treaty on European Union provides that if a Member State is the victim of armed aggression on its territory, the other Member States have an obligation of aid and assistance towards it by all the means in their power. Two qualifications follow. The clause does not prejudice the specific character of the security and defence policy of certain Member States, a phrase usually understood to protect neutral countries, and commitments under it must be consistent with those made in NATO.

France invoked the clause for the first time after the terrorist attacks in Paris in November 2015. Other Member States responded in different ways. The episode showed that the clause prescribes no procedure and no particular role for the EU institutions: it works mainly between governments. It should not be confused with the solidarity clause in Article 222 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, which covers terrorist attacks and disasters and explicitly involves the Union's own instruments.

## v04-18-c

In 1817 the economist David Ricardo set out the principle of comparative advantage with a famous example. He supposed that Portugal could produce both wine and cloth with less labour than England. Even so, he argued, both countries would gain from trade if each specialised in the good it produced at the lowest relative cost and imported the other. What matters is not whether a country is better at producing something in absolute terms, but what it gives up by producing it instead of something else: its opportunity cost.

The insight remains a cornerstone of trade theory because it shows that even a country that is less productive in every sector can trade profitably, and that the more productive country also gains. The model rests on simplifying assumptions, however, such as full employment and no transport costs. Later economists showed that, when such assumptions do not hold, trade may still raise a country's income overall while leaving particular groups of workers, regions or industries worse off.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Even a country that outperforms its partner in every sector gains from trading with it.
B. Ricardo concluded that Portugal should make both wine and cloth for itself.
C. Ricardo's reasoning was later used in Britain to justify repealing the Corn Laws.
D. Later economists found that trade usually leaves particular groups of workers worse off.

## v04-19-c

For centuries, questions of rank caused bitter disputes between diplomats. In 1661 a clash over precedence between the retinues of the Spanish and French ambassadors in London ended in bloodshed. The Congress of Vienna addressed the problem in 1815. It divided diplomatic agents into classes and provided that, within each class, precedence would depend on the date of the official notification of an envoy's arrival, not on the prestige of the country he represented.

The Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations of 1961 later codified many such customs. It makes the premises of a mission inviolable and gives diplomatic agents immunity from the host state's criminal jurisdiction and, with limited exceptions, from its civil jurisdiction. Immunity is not a licence, however. The sending state can waive it, and the host state may at any time, without giving reasons, declare a diplomat persona non grata. Because expulsions are often answered by reciprocal expulsions, governments weigh them carefully.

C. Expelling a diplomat may damage trade relations between the two states involved.
D. Diplomats are entirely immune from every kind of legal action brought in the host state.

## v04-20-c

The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, links the Mediterranean Sea with the Red Sea and allows ships travelling between Europe and Asia to avoid sailing around Africa. The alternative route via the Cape of Good Hope typically adds one to two weeks of sailing.

Its importance makes it vulnerable. In March 2021 the container ship Ever Given ran aground in the canal and blocked it for six days, holding up hundreds of vessels. From late 2023, attacks on merchant ships in the southern Red Sea led many shipping companies to send their vessels around Africa instead. Traffic through the canal fell sharply, cutting Egypt's revenue from transit fees, an important source of foreign currency for the country. The diversions showed that longer routes are not only a cost for shipowners. Because each voyage takes more time, more ships are needed to maintain the same frequency of service, which reduces available capacity and can push up freight rates even where demand has not changed.

## v04-21-c

The Antarctic Treaty was signed in Washington in 1959 by the twelve countries whose scientists had been active on the continent during the International Geophysical Year of 1957–58. Seven of them had made territorial claims in Antarctica, some of them overlapping. The Treaty did not settle these claims but froze them: nothing done while it is in force can support or deny a claim, and no new claim may be made. It reserves Antarctica for peaceful purposes, prohibiting military bases, manoeuvres and weapons testing, nuclear explosions and the disposal of radioactive waste.

More than fifty states are now parties, but decisions are taken by the Consultative Parties, which act by consensus. Besides the original signatories, only states that conduct substantial scientific research in Antarctica can acquire that status. A Protocol on Environmental Protection, signed in Madrid in 1991, designates Antarctica as a natural reserve devoted to peace and science, and prohibits any activity relating to mineral resources other than scientific research.

## v04-22-c

The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), signed in 1947, cut tariffs through successive rounds of negotiations and was built on the principle of non-discrimination. Under the most-favoured-nation rule, an advantage that a member grants to goods from one trading partner must be extended immediately to similar goods from all other members. Customs unions and free trade areas are permitted as an exception, provided they cover substantially all the trade between their members.

The World Trade Organization, which replaced the GATT's institutional framework in 1995, added a stronger system for settling disputes, in which a panel rules first and either party may appeal to a standing Appellate Body. From December 2019, however, the Appellate Body could no longer hear new appeals, because the United States had blocked the appointment of new members. In response, the European Union and other members set up a Multi-Party Interim Appeal Arbitration Arrangement, under which participants settle appeals among themselves through arbitration. It applies only to disputes between members that have joined it.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. The interim arbitration arrangement restored appeals for every dispute between members.
B. The United States blocked the appointments because the body had exceeded its mandate.
C. The GATT removed tariffs between its members altogether through its negotiating rounds.
D. A free trade area is allowed only if it covers substantially all its members' trade.
