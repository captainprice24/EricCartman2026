The Single European Act, signed in 1986 and in force from 1987, was the first major revision of the Treaties of Rome. Its central aim was to complete the internal market, meaning the free movement of goods, services, people and capital, by the end of 1992. Permafrost is ground that remains frozen for at least two consecutive years. It underlies around a quarter of the land in the Northern Hemisphere, including large parts of Siberia, Alaska and northern Canada, and it contains vast amounts of organic carbon, roughly twice as much as is currently in the atmosphere.

The term 'groupthink' was popularised by the psychologist Irving Janis in the early 1970s to describe how cohesive groups can make poor decisions because the desire for harmony and consensus overrides a realistic assessment of alternatives. ## v03-24-c

When a large enough share of a population is immune to an infectious disease, through vaccination or previous infection, the disease has difficulty spreading because each infected person passes it on to fewer than one other person on average. The share that needs to be immune depends on how contagious the disease is. Herd immunity is harder to achieve when a vaccine prevents severe illness but does less to stop transmission; such a vaccine still protects the individual, but its indirect protection of others is weaker. ## v04-18-c

In 1817 the economist David Ricardo set out the principle of comparative advantage with a famous example. He supposed that Portugal could produce both wine and cloth with less labour than England. Even so, he argued, both countries would gain from trade if each specialised in the good it produced at the lowest relative cost and imported the other. What matters is not whether a country is better at producing something in absolute terms, but what it gives up by producing it instead of something else: its opportunity cost.

The insight remains a cornerstone of trade theory because it shows that even a country that is less productive in every sector can trade profitably, and that the more productive country also gains. The model rests on simplifying assumptions, however, such as full employment and no transport costs. Later economists showed that, when such assumptions do not hold, trade may still raise a country's income overall while leaving particular groups of workers, regions or industries worse off.
