## v07-18-c

A solar cell converts light into electricity when photons free electrons in a semiconductor. Each semiconductor has a band gap, the minimum energy a photon must carry to free an electron. Photons with less energy than the band gap pass through without producing current, while photons with more energy produce current but lose the excess as heat. This trade-off sets a ceiling on efficiency.

In 1961 William Shockley and Hans-Joachim Queisser calculated this ceiling for a conventional cell made of a single semiconductor under unconcentrated sunlight: about 33%, reached with a band gap of around 1.3 to 1.4 electronvolts. Silicon, which dominates the market, has a band gap of about 1.1 electronvolts and a theoretical maximum of around 29%. The limit does not apply to tandem cells, which stack materials with different band gaps. Some, combining silicon with a perovskite layer, have exceeded 33% in the laboratory, although questions remain about how well perovskites withstand years of outdoor use.

## v07-19-c

Over the past few million years, Earth's climate has swung repeatedly between glacial periods, when ice sheets covered much of North America and northern Europe, and warmer interglacials such as the present one. In the early twentieth century Milutin Milanković proposed that these cycles were paced by regular changes in Earth's orbit and axis. The shape of the orbit varies over roughly 100,000 years. Scientists stress, however, that orbital changes act as a pacemaker: feedbacks involving carbon dioxide and the reflectivity of ice amplify their effect.

In the 1880s, two systems competed to supply electricity in the United States. Thomas Edison's companies promoted direct current (DC), which flows in one direction, while George Westinghouse, using patents licensed from Nikola Tesla, backed alternating current (AC), which reverses direction many times a second. Since the mid-twentieth century, high-voltage direct current (HVDC) links have been built where they perform better than AC, notably over very long distances and for undersea cables. Because the converter stations at each end are expensive, HVDC is generally chosen only when a link is long enough for its lower line losses to outweigh that cost.

## v08-21-c

The demographic transition model describes how populations change as societies develop. In the first stage, both birth and death rates are high and the population grows slowly, if at all. In the second, death rates fall, thanks mainly to a better food supply, sanitation and medicine, while birth rates remain high, so the population grows rapidly. In the third stage birth rates also decline, as more children survive, education spreads and families choose to have fewer children, and growth slows. In the fourth, both rates are low and the population is roughly stable.

The model was developed from the experience of Western Europe, and it describes rather than explains. Some demographers add a fifth stage for countries where fertility has fallen well below the replacement level, which in countries with low mortality is about 2.1 children per woman. In such countries deaths may eventually exceed births, and the population may decline unless immigration makes up the difference.
