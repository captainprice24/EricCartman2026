## v22-01

The European Union Solidarity Fund was created in 2002, after severe floods in central Europe. It helps Member States and accession countries cope with major natural disasters and, since 2020, also with major public health emergencies. It does not compensate individuals or businesses: it reimburses part of the public cost of emergency operations, such as restoring essential infrastructure, providing temporary accommodation and cleaning up, and insured damage is not eligible.

To count as 'major', direct damage must exceed three billion euros at 2011 prices or 0.6 per cent of the country's gross national income, whichever is lower. A government must apply within twelve weeks; the Commission then assesses the request and the Parliament and Council must approve the amount, so aid has often arrived months after the event. Advance payments were introduced to shorten the delay, but critics say the Fund still suits reconstruction better than immediate relief.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Since 2020 the Fund has covered health emergencies instead of natural disasters.
B. Most applications to the Fund have probably come from accession countries.
C. For a small economy, the threshold for a major disaster lies below three billion euros.
D. Advance payments ensure that aid now arrives immediately after a disaster.

## v22-02

The Black Death, which swept across Europe between 1347 and 1351, killed between a third and a half of the population. With so many workers dead, land was plentiful but labour scarce, and surviving peasants could demand higher wages or better terms. Rulers tried to stop this. In England, laws of 1349 and 1351 ordered wages to be held at their pre-plague levels and made it an offence to leave an employer in search of better pay. They proved hard to enforce, since employers short of hands often broke them themselves, and records suggest that real wages for many English labourers rose over the following decades.

The effects differed by region. In much of western Europe, peasants' bargaining power contributed to the decline of serfdom, whereas in parts of central and eastern Europe landowners eventually tightened their control over peasants.

A. The English wage laws did not prevent the later rise in many labourers' wages.
B. Serfdom became stronger in western Europe after the plague than before it.
C. The English laws allowed workers to leave an employer to seek better pay.
D. Labour shortages were probably worst in central and eastern Europe.

## v22-03

The human body hosts trillions of microorganisms, most of them bacteria in the gut. Textbooks long said they outnumbered human cells by ten to one, but a 2016 recalculation put the ratio closer to one to one. Gut bacteria break down dietary fibre that human enzymes cannot digest, and some produce vitamins such as vitamin K.

People with conditions such as obesity or type 2 diabetes often have a different mix of gut bacteria from healthy people. Such associations do not show which comes first: a disease, or the medicines and diet that accompany it, may change the microbiome rather than the other way round. Experiments in mice suggest a causal role, but evidence in humans is weaker. One treatment is well established: transplanting faecal bacteria from a healthy donor is highly effective against recurrent infections with Clostridioides difficile, which often flourishes after antibiotics.

A. Most of the vitamins humans need are made for them by gut bacteria.
B. Faecal transplants usually work against obesity as well as C. difficile.
C. Gut bacteria are needed to digest all of the foods that humans eat.
D. A different gut mix in sick people could be a result of their disease.

## v22-04

Iceland's volcanic geology gives it unusually easy access to underground heat. About nine in ten Icelandic homes are heated with geothermal energy, mostly through district networks that pipe hot water from boreholes to buildings. Electricity is a separate matter: most of the country's power comes from hydroelectric dams fed by glacial rivers, while geothermal plants supply roughly a quarter to a third. Almost all of Iceland's electricity and heating is therefore renewable, although transport and the fishing fleet still depend largely on imported oil. Cheap power has attracted aluminium smelters, which use the majority of the electricity generated.

Geothermal energy is not entirely free of emissions, as fluids from deep reservoirs release carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulphide. Near Reykjavík, a project captures some of these gases and injects them into basalt rock, where the carbon dioxide turns into stable minerals.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Most of the electricity generated in Iceland is used to heat homes.
B. Geothermal plants supply less of Iceland's electricity than dams do.
C. The gases captured near Reykjavík are sold to industry after capture.
D. Iceland's fishing fleet will probably switch to geothermal power soon.

## v22-05

Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken today, around 40 per cent are considered endangered. A language rarely disappears because its last speakers die suddenly; it usually fades when parents stop passing it on to their children, often because another language offers better access to education or work. Once a generation grows up without it, the language is unlikely to recover without deliberate effort.

Revival is difficult but not impossible. Hebrew, used for centuries mainly in religious and scholarly writing, became the everyday language of millions in the twentieth century, and Welsh has benefited from its use in schools and broadcasting. Manx, the language of the Isle of Man, lost its last native speaker in 1974, yet a small community now speaks it again after learning it through classes and a Manx-medium primary school. Such cases depend on sustained public support and on families choosing to use the language at home.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Most endangered languages vanish when their last speakers die suddenly.
B. Revival can succeed for any language as long as schools teach it.
C. A language with no native speakers left is not always lost for good.
D. Public support alone was enough to bring Manx back into use.

## v22-06

Prescribed burning, the deliberate setting of low-intensity fires under controlled conditions, aims to reduce the dry grass, shrubs and dead wood that let wildfires grow large. Aboriginal Australians used fire to manage land for thousands of years, and foresters in parts of the United States have relied on it for decades. In southern Europe it is less common, although Portugal and Spain have expanded its use after a series of deadly fires, as abandoned farmland has filled with shrubs and young forest. Many Mediterranean plants are also adapted to periodic fire and regenerate quickly.

Burning is not risk-free. It can be carried out only in narrow windows of suitable weather, when wind, temperature and humidity keep the fire manageable, and those windows may become shorter as summers lengthen. Smoke affects nearby towns, and on rare occasions a prescribed fire escapes control.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Portugal and Spain may now burn more land deliberately than the United States.
B. Mediterranean plants recover from every fire within a few months.
C. Prescribed burning removes the risk of large wildfires where it is used.
D. Longer summers could leave fewer suitable days for prescribed burning.

## v22-07

Printing with movable type is often credited to Johannes Gutenberg, who produced his Bible in Mainz around 1455, but the technique had been invented earlier in East Asia. In the eleventh century the Chinese artisan Bi Sheng made individual characters from baked clay, and in 1377 Korean monks printed the Jikji, the oldest surviving book printed with movable metal type.

One explanation for printing's faster impact in Europe lies in writing systems. Chinese requires thousands of distinct characters, so for many purposes carving a whole page on a wooden block remained more practical, whereas a European alphabet needed only a few dozen letters. Gutenberg also combined several innovations, including a hand mould for casting identical letters and an oil-based ink. Historians add that printing's impact depended on markets, literacy and the religious conflicts that created demand for pamphlets.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. Metal movable type was used in Korea before Gutenberg printed his Bible.
B. Bi Sheng cast his movable characters from metal in the eleventh century.
C. Korean printers probably gave up movable type because metal was costly.
D. The alphabet alone explains why printing changed Europe faster than China.

## v22-08

In the hot summer of 1858, the smell from the River Thames, into which London's sewage drained, became so overpowering that it was called the Great Stink. At the time many doctors believed that cholera spread through foul air, or 'miasma'; the physician John Snow had argued in 1854 that it spread through contaminated water, but his view was not yet widely accepted. Ironically, it was fear of the smell, rather than an understanding of water-borne disease, that persuaded Parliament to act.

Within weeks it funded a system designed by the engineer Joseph Bazalgette, whose intercepting sewers carried waste eastwards to be released downstream of the city. Much of the network was completed in the 1860s and 1870s. When cholera struck London in 1866, the worst-affected district was one not yet connected to the new sewers, which helped convince sceptics that the water theory was correct.

Which of the following statements is correct?

A. John Snow's water theory persuaded Parliament to fund the sewers in 1858.
B. Parliament's quick decision may have rested on a theory later shown wrong.
C. The new sewers released London's waste into the Thames above the city.
D. The 1866 outbreak convinced every London doctor that cholera was water-borne.

## v22-09

In May 1997 IBM's Deep Blue defeated the world chess champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match, a year after Kasparov had won their first encounter. Deep Blue relied above all on speed: special chips let it examine some 200 million positions per second, and it scored them with an evaluation function tuned with the help of grandmasters. It did not learn from experience; its knowledge of chess had largely been supplied by its designers.

Two decades later, a program called AlphaZero, given only the rules of chess, taught itself by playing millions of games against itself and soon outperformed the strongest conventional engines, while examining far fewer positions per second. Many players described its style as more intuitive and more willing to sacrifice material for long-term advantage. Today even programs on ordinary phones can beat the best humans, and professionals use them routinely to prepare.

B. Professional players generally prefer AlphaZero's style to other engines'.
C. Unlike Deep Blue, AlphaZero began with no chess knowledge beyond the rules.
D. Deep Blue's designers supplied all of the chess knowledge it used.

## v22-10

The Svalbard Global Seed Vault, opened in 2008 on a Norwegian Arctic island, stores duplicate samples of seeds held by gene banks around the world as a backup. Seeds are kept at minus eighteen degrees in chambers cut into a mountain, where permafrost would keep them frozen for some time even if cooling failed. Norway owns the facility, but the depositing gene banks retain ownership of their seeds, and only they can withdraw them; samples are not tested or used for research at Svalbard itself.

The first withdrawal came in 2015, when an agricultural research centre that had moved its headquarters from Aleppo because of the war in Syria retrieved duplicates to rebuild its collections in Lebanon and Morocco. In 2017 meltwater entered the entrance tunnel; no seeds were affected, but Norway made the tunnel more watertight.
