## v03-01-c

The Single European Act, signed in 1986 and in force from 1987, was the first major revision of the Treaties of Rome. Its central aim was to complete the internal market, meaning the free movement of goods, services, people and capital, by the end of 1992. A Commission White Paper published in 1985 had identified around 300 measures needed to remove physical, technical and fiscal barriers between the Member States.

To make this possible, the Act extended qualified majority voting in the Council to most single-market legislation, which had often required unanimity; tax matters, however, continued to require unanimity. It also strengthened the European Parliament through a new 'cooperation procedure'. Not every barrier disappeared by the deadline: many measures had been adopted by the end of 1992, but some were transposed late, and in areas such as services and public procurement the market remained incomplete for years. Even so, the programme is widely credited with reviving European integration.

In 1965 France withdrew its representatives from most Council meetings, leaving its seat empty for about six months. The immediate dispute concerned the financing of the Common Agricultural Policy, but it was entangled with Commission proposals to give the Community its own resources and the European Parliament greater budgetary powers, and with the planned move to qualified majority voting in the Council for many decisions from 1966.

The Working Time Directive sets minimum requirements for the organisation of working time in the EU. ## v03-21-c

Permafrost is ground that remains frozen for at least two consecutive years. It underlies around a quarter of the land in the Northern Hemisphere, including large parts of Siberia, Alaska and northern Canada, and it contains vast amounts of organic carbon, roughly twice as much as is currently in the atmosphere.

As the climate warms, permafrost is thawing, and microbes decompose the previously frozen material, releasing carbon dioxide and methane. Whether the thawed ground releases more of one gas or the other depends largely on conditions: in waterlogged soils with little oxygen, more methane is produced. This can create a feedback loop, in which warming causes thawing that releases gases causing further warming. Scientists expect these emissions to occur gradually over decades to centuries rather than as a sudden pulse, although abrupt thaw can occur locally. Thawing also damages buildings, roads and pipelines, and in some Arctic towns the cost of repairing infrastructure has already risen sharply.

## v03-22-c

The term 'groupthink' was popularised by the psychologist Irving Janis in the early 1970s to describe how cohesive groups can make poor decisions because the desire for harmony and consensus overrides a realistic assessment of alternatives. Janis studied foreign policy failures such as the Bay of Pigs invasion of 1961, and contrasted them with cases in which similar groups had decided well, such as the handling of the Cuban missile crisis the following year.

Symptoms include an illusion of invulnerability, pressure on members who express doubts, self-censorship and an illusion of unanimity, sometimes reinforced by 'mindguards' who shield the leader from unwelcome information. Janis argued that the risk was greatest when a cohesive group was insulated from outside opinion, led by someone who promoted a preferred solution, and under high stress. He recommended inviting outside experts and assigning someone to act as a 'devil's advocate'. Later researchers have found mixed evidence for the model, but many of his recommendations are still widely used in organisations.

## v03-24-c

When a large enough share of a population is immune to an infectious disease, through vaccination or previous infection, the disease has difficulty spreading because each infected person passes it on to fewer than one other person on average. This effect, known as herd immunity, protects people who cannot be vaccinated, such as very young babies.

The share that needs to be immune depends on how contagious the disease is. For measles, one of the most contagious diseases known, around 95% of people need to be immune to prevent outbreaks; for less contagious diseases the threshold is lower. Immunity must also be spread fairly evenly: if unvaccinated people are concentrated in particular communities or schools, outbreaks can occur there even when national coverage looks high. Herd immunity is harder to achieve when a vaccine prevents severe illness but does less to stop transmission; such a vaccine still protects the individual, but its indirect protection of others is weaker. Authorities therefore monitor coverage locally as well as nationally.
